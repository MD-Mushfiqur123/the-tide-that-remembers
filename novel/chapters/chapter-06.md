# CHAPTER SIX — Open

Dawn on the canal-end came up the color of the wax in the Ledger hall, and the opening team arrived with the light: three senior Wardens, an auditor, a portable slate-rig, and Yuki Ashihara in a coat that was still too big for her, carrying a mask that had never been worn.

Ren was not supposed to be there. He was there anyway, at the fishmonger's awning, palms closed by order — *palms closed, listener, this is Warden work* — and the team let him stay because the auditor, a dry woman named Okone with a voice like a closing drawer, had decided that a documented listener was cheaper than an undocumented one.

"Sequence," Okone said, to the team, to the square, to the morning. "Cadet takes the mask. Cadet makes the cut. We hold the perimeter, we log the release, the auditor certifies the pool as cleared. Four breaths of work." She looked at the flat water with the expression of a woman reading a tide-table that had never once been wrong. "Second-layer pool, no depth, resident four days at most. The smallest opening in the district ledger this year. Cadet — the mask."

Yuki took the mask off her hip.

Ren had seen masks in pictures, in the oath-day processions, grey-white and ceremonial. He had never seen one *held*. In Yuki's hands it changed: the weight of it went into her wrists, her stance dropped, her shoulders settled, and for one moment the cadet and the coat and the too-big everything of her were gone, and what stood at the edge of the canal-end was the thing the order had been building for two hundred years.

The mask went on. It had no eyeholes. It did not need them. Wardens who wore the mask were not looking at the Hollow; they were looking at the *wound*, and the wound did not have a direction.

"Beginning," Yuki said, from behind the mask, in a voice that was not quite hers — flatter, ruled, the voice the mask does the breathing for.

She raised her hands — palms open, the listener's shape, and Ren felt the wrongness of that land in his teeth: they had taken the one gesture the city used for mercy and drilled it into a tool — and the flat water of the canal-end *rose*.

---

It did not surge. It lifted — a column of canal water, grey and seamless, standing up out of the canal-end into the dawn with no sound but a sound like a hall of drawers breathing in — and inside the column, the pool's shape assembled itself: the shame of a street, four days pooled, a hundred small debts and silences and doors closed gently in faces, and all of it wearing, as the Hollow in the market had worn it, a door.

But this door was different.

The market Hollow's door had been shut. This one — the whole square saw it, the team saw it, the auditor's stylus stopped moving — this door was *open*, a hand's width, and through the gap came the humming, thin and off-key and badly, joyfully patient, the six-year-old's tune, the kitchen tune, the tune that had been singing under the thirteenth bell — except now, in the dawn, with the column standing, Ren could hear what he had refused to hear in the dark:

The humming was not coming from the door.

It was coming from *behind* it. From further down, from deeper in the pool, the way a voice comes from a room beyond a room — and the door was not the Hollow's face. The door was the Hollow's *back*. The thing in the canal-end had been standing between the city and something else, facing the wrong way, holding the gap shut with its shoulders, and the humming came through the gap it was guarding, and the tune had words under it again, and the words were not *brother, stay*.

The words were: *don't find me.*

"Cadet," Okone said, and her drawer-voice had a crack in it, thin as a thread. "The pattern. Read the pattern."

Yuki's sensor — the whitebait bottle, set on the cobbles at the column's edge — had gone still. Not calm. *Still*: the fish floated nose-down, dead since before dawn, it would be found, and every instrument on the rig pointed not at the column but past it, down-canal, the way compasses point.

"The pool's resident isn't the pool," Yuki said, from behind the mask, and the flat voice had the cadet in it now, fighting through. "The pool's a — a *screen*. The shame pooled over something moving under it. The humming pattern's sixth-order, child, female, and it's *receding* — it's been receding since the bell, it used the pool the way a—" the flat voice failed altogether, and for one sentence the cadet's own came through the mask, sixteen years old and aghast: "It used the opening as cover. We're not opening a Hollow. We're opening a *decoy*."

"Cut it," Okone said.

"Superintendent—"

"*Cut it, cadet.* The pool is logged, the schedule is running, and the district does not hold a resident past its window. Cut it."

And Yuki Ashihara, nineteen years old, top of her intake, holder of umbrellas, cut it.

---

The manual's opening was four breaths, and it took four breaths, and it was clean, and it was the worst thing Ren had ever watched.

The mask did its work: the column of water unfolded along a line no eye could follow, and the door-shape came apart the way a knot comes apart when the right thread is pulled — gently, completely, irreversibly — and the pool's contents released into the dawn air as spray, as light, as a hundred small unfinished feelings going up at once: grief-drift, first-layer now, harmless, spent, a street's shame returned to the rain it came from.

The Hollow did not scream. Litter doesn't scream. It came apart with the sound the hall made, breathing, and the last of it — the shoulders, the guarding shape, the thing that had stood with its back to the city holding a gap shut — dissolved with its door half-open, guarding nothing, having guarded, for four days, a departure.

The spray drifted up into the morning. The team stood in it. The auditor's stylus moved again. And in the crowd beyond the rope — the canal-end crowd, the debt-row crowd, the people whose shame had pooled — a woman cried out.

Ren knew her. The whole district knew her: O-Tama, who kept the pickle stall two rows off the canal-end, sixty years old, a laugh like a dropped bucket, a stall that had never once in living memory been closed. She was at the rope with her hands over her mouth, and the spray of the opened Hollow was drifting through her, through everyone, a hundred spent feelings going up — and one of them, one thread in the hundred, *found* her.

Ren saw it happen. Saw the thread — grey, small, second-layer, a shame the size of a coin — come down out of the spray like a leaf and land on her, and *root*, and O-Tama folded over her stall as if she had been struck, and began, in front of the whole square, in front of the auditor and the team and the mask, to weep.

"Rooting," the auditor said, stylus moving, certifying. "Return-thread. Minor. Expected in four percent of domestic openings." She wrote the number. She did not look at the woman. "Pool certified cleared. Cadet — mask off. Well executed."

Yuki took the mask off.

Her face underneath was the color of the dawn, and her eyes were on O-Tama, on the thread that had rooted in her, on the weeping that the manual called a return-thread, *minor, expected* — and Ren, palms closed by order, standing at the awning, read it anyway, because he could not help it, because it was the only thread in the square still moving:

*A kitchen, forty years ago, morning. A young woman, six months heavy, standing at a canal-end in the rain, and the Tide is speaking to her — the first Tide, the waking, the offer — because her mother has died in the night and the grief is too big to hold: the Tide will take it, says the offer, all of it, the whole weight, and the young woman — who is O-Tama, who is sixty now, who has laughed like a dropped bucket for forty years — says yes, says take it, says it twice, and the Tide takes, and it takes not the grief she offered but the grief she did not: it takes the child. The one that would have been. The name she had already chosen, the morning-sickness mornings, the stool by the counter she would have needed, the whole unfinished person — paid, whole, gone — and for forty years she has not known what she was missing, and the ledger calls it an asset class, and the rain has kept it, and this morning a cadet with a mask opened a pool two streets from her stall, and one grey coin-sized thread of it came home, and it is the *wanting*, only the wanting, the wanting came back and nothing else, and she is weeping at a pickle stall for a child she chose to forget in the rain forty years ago, and the child was never born, and the wanting is the only part of the child that was ever real, and it is rooting in her like a lamp being lit in an empty room—*

The thread finished. Went out. O-Tama straightened up slowly at her stall, an old woman with wet eyes and no memory of why, and began, with the terrible competence of the long-practiced, to rearrange her pickles.

---

They certified the pool at the seventh bell. The team packed the rig. Okone signed the slate with the closing-drawer voice: *opening complete, release logged, return-threads: one, minor.* And Yuki Ashihara, coat too big, mask in its case, stood at the canal-end steps and looked at the flat water where a door had guarded a departure, and did not move for a long time.

"Ishikari," she said at last, without turning. "The manual. Say it to me."

"The manual says Hollows are litter."

"The manual says the release *returns* the memories." Her voice was very quiet. "It says the rain gives them back. It says the crowd feels lighter. I've written that sentence on an exam. I've *graded* that sentence." She turned, then, and her face was not the dawn anymore; it was the archive's face, the face of someone who has read the pre-Ledger files and could not sleep. "It doesn't give them back, Ishikari. It *scatters* them. A hundred feelings went up this morning and one came home by accident, to the wrong woman, forty years late, wanting-only — and the auditor *logged it as a success*." Her hand went to her slate, live, official, the rig's record of the morning. "O-Tama. Pickle stall. The child. The name — the name she chose, we could find it, it'd be in the old district rolls, we could—" and she stopped, because they both heard it, the thing underneath the sentence, the thing the whole order was built on top of:

*You cannot give back what the system has spent. You can only log it.*

That night, in her rented room above the bridge, Yuki Ashihara took a new slate — cheap, grey, unregistered, the kind the debt-rows used for shopping lists — and wrote, in a hand that had not yet learned to shake but was learning:

*Second Ledger. Entry one. O-Tama, third district, pickle stall. Tithe paid: forty years ago, consentless, misdirected — one child, unborn, whole. Returned this date: one (1) wanting. Balance outstanding: everything.*

She looked at the entry for a long time. Then she added a column header, at the top of the empty slate, in letters small enough to hide:

*Resolved: no.*

And at the canal-end, at dusk, the flat water lay under the Evening Rain with nothing in it at all — no door, no pool, no humming, the sister-Hollow gone down-canal, down-city, out into two hundred years of rain with a departure for a cover — and Ren Ishikari stood at the steps with his palms open, alone, listening to the district's small griefs go up around him like sparks, and thought, with the calm of a man standing on a rail line feeling the first vibration through his boots:

*She's been hiding in the city's shame for four years. And this morning, we drained the pool she was hiding behind.*

The Evening Rain fell. Somewhere down-canal, in the dark, in the debt-rows, in the low places where the city kept what it did not look at, something unfinished began — softly, off-key, badly, joyfully — to look for a new pool.
