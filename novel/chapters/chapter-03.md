# CHAPTER THREE — What the Ledger Buys

The Ledger hall of Kagemori had been built by people who believed that grief had a shape, and that the shape was a drawer.

There were eleven million drawers.

They lined the walls of the great vaulted hall from floor to the ceiling's grey glass, brass-fronted, each one stamped with a district number and a family mark, and the whole of them together made a sound Ren had never heard anywhere else in the world — a sound like rain heard through a wall, a sound like a city breathing in a sleep it had agreed on. The drawers were never all closed. Somewhere in the hall, at any moment, a thousand of them were open a finger's width, because somewhere in Kagemori a Tithe was being paid, or checked, or mourned, and the hall kept them open until the ink dried.

The ink was the smell. Wax and rain and something older under both, the smell of two hundred years of entries, and Ren stood in the doorway of it at the ninth bell with his palms closed — palms closed, in here; the hall was the one place in the city where open palms were against the law — and understood why Yuki had said *the hall doesn't like listeners*.

It wasn't that the hall didn't like them. It was that in the hall, a listener with open palms would hear the whole city at once, and the hall had been designed, two hundred years ago, by people who knew exactly what that would do to a person.

"Palms closed," said the door-warden, who was ninety if she was a day, and had said the two words so many times they had worn smooth as river stones. "Welcome to the Ledger, where what you lost is kept safe from you."

---

Yuki was waiting in the public gallery with two visitor slates and a face that was doing something complicated with the word *voluntarily*.

"Rule one," she said, handing him a slate. "We're here to file a witness addendum on the market Hollow. That's the file the hall will see. Rule two." She lowered her voice, and the gallery's acoustics took it and filed it somewhere above the drawers. "Your entry. Ishikari, Ren. Third district. I pulled the index last night. It exists, it's current, it's *paid* — and the Tithe field is sealed. Not redacted. Sealed. There's a difference, and the difference is a seal I have never seen, and I've read the seal registry."

"What's the registry say?"

"Nothing. That's the registry's entry for your seal: *nothing*. A blank line in a book of seals." She said it the way she said tide-tables, but her hand wasn't steady on the slate, and Ren noted it the way she'd noted his open palms: filed, for later. "W-9," she said. "Two characters, no registry, on a seventeen-year-old's file. I want to see it with my own eyes, and then I want to never think about it again, and I suspect I'll get exactly one of those."

They went down into the drawers.

---

The Ishikari drawer was in the third district row, at the height of a standing man's heart, which the door-warden said was a coincidence and which Yuki said was never a coincidence, not in this hall.

The drawer's front was brass, green at the edges, stamped with the family mark: a wave over a roofline. Ren had never thought about the mark. It had just been the shape on their letters, their ration chits, his mother's medicine ledger. A wave over a roofline. He was seventeen, and standing in front of two hundred years of his family's paid grief, and he was noticing, for the first time, that the wave in the mark was drawn *falling away* from the roof, not toward it.

Yuki opened the drawer with her Warden key, and the hall's breathing-sound changed pitch around them, the way a room changes pitch when a door is opened onto a room that didn't know it was being listened to.

Inside: slates. Thin ones, wax-faced, one per entry, filed by year. Ren's mother's — he saw the year, and did not touch it, and the not-touching cost more than touching would have. His own, at the back, the newest, and across its wax face the entry in the hall's ruled hand:

*ISHIKARI, REN. Tithe paid, aged thirteen. Authority: self, distressed. Witnessed: yes. Asset class:* 

And then the seal.

It was not ink. Ren had expected a redaction, a black line, the hall's way of closing a door. This was not a closing. The wax of the slate had *moved* — the entry's last words sank as he watched, the surface rippling once, twice, and settling into a raised shape the size of a thumbnail:

A door.

A tiny, closed, grey door, standing in the wax of his own Ledger entry, and around it, for a finger's width in every direction, the wax had gone flat and featureless, the way the canal-end water had gone flat, the way the Glass was said to be flat.

"Don't touch it," Yuki said. Her voice had the ledger line back, but underneath it, for the second time in two days, the cadet. "Seals with *geometry* aren't Warden work. Geometry means Depth. Fourth, at least — you need to shape the rain to shape wax like this, and Fourth-Depth work on a citizen's file means it was done from *above* the file." She was writing without looking at her slate, which was a Warden trick, and her hand was worse than unsteady now. "Ishikari. Files above the file. There is nothing above the file. The Marshal answers to the Ledger. The Ledger answers to itself. That's the whole roof."

"Then who's holding the pen?"

"That's what I came to find out, and I'm starting to wish I'd stayed at the academy." She looked at the little door a moment longer. "It's shut," she said, half to herself, in the voice you use for a thing you're checking against a list you keep in your chest. "It's a door and it's *shut*, and it's been shut four years, and the wax around it hasn't healed. Wax heals. Everything in this hall heals. Whatever shut this one—"

"—is still holding it," Ren said.

They stood in the hall's breathing-sound, in front of a drawer at the height of his heart, and neither of them said the next thing, which was that a door shut from the other side was also a door that could be knocked on from it.

---

The old clerk found them there.

He appeared the way the hall's staff appeared, out of the aisles, with the soundless tread of people who had spent their lives among things that must not be startled. He was small, and dry, and his hall uniform was so old the grey had gone the color of the wax, and he looked at the sealed entry for a long time — at the little door — with an expression Ren could not read until he realized it was not an expression. It was the *absence* of one. A face that had paid, over a long career, whatever it cost to keep looking at things like this and reporting nothing.

"W-9," the clerk said. "You'll want to know what it is. Everyone does. The registry's blank because it isn't ours." He said it to the drawer, not to them. "Wardens seal with authority. This was sealed with *payment*. Someone bought the silence on this entry, and the hall took the fee, and the hall does not ask its customers what they are."

"Who sells silence above the Ledger?" Yuki said.

"Child," the clerk said, with the gentleness of the very tired, "there is one market above the Ledger, and one vendor, and you've seen his boat." He turned the drawer's key himself, and the little door sank back into flat wax as the brass front closed, and Ren felt it go the way you feel a lamp go out in a far room. "The hall's advice — mine, not the hall's, the hall has no advice, the hall has *rates* —" he looked at Ren then, the whole of his dry face, "— is that a Tithe paid is a question answered. Whatever you bought at thirteen, you bought it from something that does not do refunds. Most people live longer not knowing. The ones who find out—" he glanced, once, at the drawer, "—usually wish they'd bought silence too."

He left them the way he'd come, soundless, and the hall breathed around them, eleven million drawers, a city's worth of grief kept safe from its owners.

---

They said almost nothing on the walk back through the evening streets. At the mouth of Ren's lane, Yuki stopped, and stood with her slate under her arm, and looked at the rain coming down — the ordinary, harmless, first-layer dusk rain, grief-thin, drifting up out of the district like steam off a warm thing.

"Ishikari," she said. "The market Hollow. The humming. The child's pattern." The ruled voice, the ledger line, expecting a figure. "You said you don't know who she is."

"I did."

"And the seal on your file is four years old." She looked at him, and Ren watched her not ask the question — watched the oath and the cadet and the coat all argue about it and lose. "Tithe paid, aged thirteen. Asset class sealed. A door in the wax." A breath. "Whatever you paid," she said, "the hall thinks it's still holding the door shut on it. And the Hollow in the canal is humming the tune of whatever's behind the door."

She left him with that, at the mouth of his lane, in the thin rain, and she never learned — not for three hundred and sixty-six more chapters — that she had just said the whole novel out loud in a doorway, and that neither of them had heard it.

Ren stood in his lane until her coat was gone, and then he did the thing he had not done in four years: he put his palms open, in the rain, alone, and waited for the dusk to leak him.

One thread came out. Thin, pale, small — the kind of thread the rain usually took without comment, a nothing, a crumb. It rose off him and unfolded in the air, and Ren read it, because it was his, and he had paid so much, and it was all he had left of the payment:

*A kitchen, low-ceilinged, morning. A girl on a stool, cracking eggs with one hand, humming — off-key, badly, joyfully. She is humming for someone in the doorway. The someone is holding the doorframe the way you hold a thing you are saying goodbye to. The girl does not look up. She is six, and the eggs matter, and she is humming, and the someone in the doorway is thinking, with the whole of his thirteen-year-old heart: stay. Stay, stay, stay—*

The thread snapped, and was gone upward, spent.

Ren stood in his lane with his palms open and his face wet, and it was not the rain.
