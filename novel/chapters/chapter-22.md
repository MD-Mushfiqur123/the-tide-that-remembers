# Chapter Twenty-Two — The Drowned City

The straight line home to Kagemori crossed the shallows, and the shallows had a name that no chart printed anymore, because the Guild had struck it from the index two hundred years ago on the grounds that a city that is under the sea is not a city, it is a *wreck*, and the Guild did not wreck-index, it wreck-*ignored*.

The boatmen called it the Drowned City anyway. The boatmen called it a great deal of things that never made it into indexes.

"Rules of the crossing," the oldest ferryman at the last Salt Road station had said, when Yuki bought the depth-tables with the last of the Saint's salt-bread money. He had laid them out flat, like knives. "One: cross at slack water, and only at slack water — the drowned keep their own hours, and at any other tide the streets *walk*. Two: whatever knocks on the hull, and something will, you do not answer it in words. Three — " and here he had looked at Ren, at Ren's open hands, at whatever it was boatmen saw when they looked at this particular boy, and he had lowered his voice as if the water outside the station were listening, "— three: if you see a light below, it is a finished memory, and it is *where it should be*, and you leave it burning. The drowned city is lit, child. It has been lit for two hundred years. It is the only city in the world that finished its grieving, and you do not *look* at a thing like that too hard, or it will finish yours."

They crossed at slack water, at dawn, in a rain so thin it was barely a decision the sky was making, and the Drowned City came up under the hull like a face coming up under a sheet.

---

It was whole. That was the first wrongness, and it never stopped being wrong: not a wreck — a *city*. Streets in grid and curve, a harbor district with its mooring-stones standing, a hill of small houses climbing to a citadel, a market street with its awnings folded down for a night two centuries long. The Sundering had not broken it. The Sundering had *lowered* it — set it down under the sea the way you set a sleeping child into bed, carefully, completely, with the doors shut and the lamps out.

Except the lamps were not out.

The light came up from below in the way that light comes from lanterns — warm, stationary, *placed* — and there were thousands of them, hanging in the drowned streets where lanterns hang, in windows where lanterns sit, on hooks by doors where lanterns wait for someone to come home, and every one of them burned underwater, calmly, without flickering, and the light of them made the whole city visible from the surface as a *glow*, a city-shaped aurora lying on the seabed, and Ren stood at the rail with both hands on the rail and understood the Lantern Island's deepest rule from the wrong direction:

Finished memories become lanterns. The Lantern Island *harvested* them from the living.

The drowned city had grown its own.

Two hundred years of finishing. Two hundred years of a drowned remnant — whoever the Sundering had left below, and the reports said no one was left below, and the reports were wrong — two hundred years of whatever souls the sea had kept, finishing, one by one, the things the water had interrupted: the letter, the kite, the confession, the goodbye. And every finishing had become a light. And the light had stayed. And the city that the index called a wreck was the best-lit place in the world.

"It's beautiful," Kaito said, at the rail, chart forgotten in his hands. "It's the most beautiful thing I've ever charted, and I will never put it on a map, because if I put it on a map they'll come, and they'll *harvest* it, and the Guild will call the harvest a survey." He said it flatly, the way the ship said flat things. "Some places should be wrong on every chart."

The bilge had been humming since dawn. It hummed differently here — Ren had noticed it first, an hour back, the tune gaining something, an undertow of *recognition*, the way a person hums louder walking toward a house they lived in before they could talk. And the hum had a bearing. Kaito noticed it second, because Kaito noticed directions; Yuki third, because Yuki noticed everything and admitted things last.

"It's pulling," Ren said. "The tune. It's not just humming. It's *pointing*."

They followed the pointing. The Wrong Way threaded the drowned streets' upper air at slack water, slow, with Ryudo at the stern watching the wake and the masked nine nowhere in the rain, and the glow came up brighter street by street, and the humming came up stronger, and it led them — of course it led them, it had always been going to lead them — to the hill of small houses, and up the hill, to a kitchen.

---

You could see it through the water. That was the wrongness that never stopped: the water over the city was clear as Glass, clear as *glass*, and the kitchen's window faced up, and the kitchen was lit — one lantern, on the table, the warmest light in the whole drowned constellation — and it was *furnished*. Table. Stools. A stove with a kettle on it. A shelf of bowls. On the wall, pencil marks climbing a doorframe: heights, dates, a child measured through years, and beside the marks a small board, painted, with a name on it.

The name was not visible. The board was not blank — that was different from blank; blank is absence, and the board was *paid*, the letters gone the way a Tithe goes, and where the name had been the wood was smooth and pale, like a scar, like a page in a registry where a seal should be.

And on the table: three places, set. Three bowls. Three cups. And the food in the bowls was steaming — steam rising through water, in threads, upward, unhurried, the way steam rises in a kitchen in the morning when the morning is being *kept* —

"Someone's home," Ryudo said, from the stern, very quietly, and did not turn around, because rule two was about knocks and he had invented a rule three of his own, years ago, on other water: *some doors you do not watch open.*

The hull drifted to a stop over the kitchen, at slack water, in the thin rain, and below them the lantern burned on the set table, and the steam rose, and the bilge hummed the tune — complete now, both parts, the whole kitchen's morning — and Ren looked down through the clear water at the pale scar on the name-board, and his hands, his open, listener's hands, closed for the first time in the saga, into fists, entirely on their own.

Because he knew the kitchen.

Not from memory — from *music*. The counter-tune. His mother's counter-tune, the harmony she sang against his sister's humming, the part that made the humming music, the tune she had sung in *their* kitchen, in Kagemori, in a house with a different hill and a different sea — the same notes, note for note, and a tune does not survive two hundred years and two hundred miles note for note unless it is *carried*, hand to hand, mother to daughter, kitchen to kitchen, down a family like an heirloom.

His family had come from here.

His family had come from *here*, and the tune had come with them, and the height-marks on the doorframe were in a hand that made his fists want to open, and the pale paid name on the board was in the same alphabet as the blank line in a registry beside the seal W-9, and below the hull, in the dark, at the edge of the glow, the whistling thing had stopped walking — stopped, for the first time in four hundred miles, *stopped* — and stood, in the dark, at the edge of the light of the finished city, like a man at the edge of his own front garden, and did not come in.

And the kitchen door, below, in the drowned street, opened.
