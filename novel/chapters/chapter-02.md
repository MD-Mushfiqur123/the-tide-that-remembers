# CHAPTER TWO — The Grey Coat

The crack in the sky lasted exactly as long as a held breath.

Ren knew, because he had been holding one. The white line burned horizon to horizon, thin as a thread, and the whole market's leaked memories turned upward toward it like iron filings, and then Ren blinked — he would always believe, afterward, that it was the blinking — and the sky was only the sky, grey and ordinary, raining.

The Evening Rain went on falling as if nothing had happened. That was the terrible part. Not the crack. The *after* of the crack: two hundred people standing in a market with their palms open and their faces tilted up, waiting for the world to acknowledge what it had just done, and the world declining, politely, to have done anything at all.

"Refraction," said the crier at the harbor-end of the square, an hour later, in the flat voice the criers used for official corrections. "The Wardens confirm a refraction event. Light on ice-cloud. No cause for alarm. The market reopens at the night bell."

Around the square, people nodded the way people nod at weather. Ren watched them do it and understood, in one cold drop, how a city forgets on schedule. You did not need to take their memories. You only needed to give them a smaller word than the thing itself, and *refraction* was a very small word, and the crack had been the biggest thing any of them would ever see.

The Hollow was gone.

That was the other thing the corrections did not mention. Ren had seen it go — the column of wrong rain collapsing inward like a held note, the door-face folding shut, the whole shape of it sliding into the canal-end water without a splash, the way a name slides out of a sentence. The Wardens' patrol arrived forty minutes after the crack, which was thirty-nine minutes after the Hollow left, and they found: wet cobbles, a dropped barrel of mackerel, and four hundred witnesses who remembered, with the city's practiced softness, almost nothing.

They found one witness who remembered everything. That was the problem.

---

She came to the fishmonger's stall at the second bell, and Ren knew she was Warden before he saw the coat, because the whole market *changed pitch* around her — the way a room changes when a bill collector walks in, everyone suddenly aware of what they owe.

The coat was grey wool, regulation, and it was a size too big for her, in the way of a coat inherited from someone who had died in it. The white mask hung at her hip, on its hook, face-down — Wardens wore the mask only for openings, and a cadet with a mask on her hip was a cadet who had never opened anything, and knew it, and had decided the coat would do the worrying for her. Her slate was out. The slate was not.

"You're the listener," she said. Not a question. She had a voice like a ledger line: short, ruled, and expecting a figure.

"Listeners don't have names?" Ren said.

"They have entries." She turned the slate toward him. On it, his district file, and his file was longer than a seventeen-year-old's file had any right to be, and part of it was sealed — he saw the seal and something in his chest went very still, the way water goes still when something large passes underneath. "Ren Ishikari. Licensed listener, third district, evening market, eleven years' standing."

"I'm seventeen."

"Then you started at six, which is illegal, which is why your file is interesting." She said it without heat, the way you'd note a tide-table. "You were at the canal-end when the Hollow stood up."

"Everyone was at the canal-end."

"Everyone *ran* from the canal-end. The barrel-boy says you walked toward it. Palms open." She looked at his hands, then, briefly, at his face, and Ren watched her do the thing Wardens were trained to do, which was weigh a citizen for tithable material. "Why?"

"Because it was between the Hollow and the market," Ren said. "And I'm faster than the market."

It was a joke. She didn't smile, but something in her jaw moved, the way a ledger line moves when a figure doesn't fit the column. "The Hollow took no one," she said. "First Hollow in this district in six years, it stood in a crowded square for the length of four breaths, and it took no one. It looked at the crowd, and it looked at you, and it left. Openings aren't supposed to work that way. Hollows don't *choose*."

"This one did."

"Hollows can't choose. They're not alive. They're—" She stopped herself, and Ren watched the oath land: *they're unfinished*, was what the manual said, *they're the rain's litter, they're nothing*, was what the oath made you say. She was a cadet. The oath was still wet on her. "The canal-end," she said instead. "Show me."

---

The canal-end at dusk-next was sealed with rope and Warden tape, and the rain came down on it harder than on the rest of the market, which was wrong, and both of them knew it was wrong, and neither of them said it.

Yuki — her name was Yuki Ashihara, she said it once, at the rope, like a formality she resented needing — set her instruments on the wet cobbles: a slate, a tuning-fork of grey metal, and a bottle of rainwater with a fish in it. Ren looked at the fish.

"It's a rain-sensor," she said. "It's a whitebait. It swims toward concentration. Don't make the face."

"What face?"

"The face the fish is making."

The canal-end water was flat. Not calm — *flat*, the way the Glass was said to be flat, no ripple, no reflection, the dusk refusing to land on it. Yuki knelt at the edge with her fork and struck it once against the cobble, and the tone went out over the water and came back wrong: doubled, dragged, arriving after the strike with a delay that had nowhere to come from.

"It's still here," she said quietly. Not to Ren. To the slate, which was recording. "Not — present. *Resident.* The water's holding its shape. Whatever stood up in this square didn't leave. It *poured itself* into the canal, and the canal is keeping it, and—" the fork's tone doubled again, and this time the doubling had a rhythm to it, a rise and fall, off-key, badly, joyfully, and Yuki stopped writing.

Ren had stopped breathing.

The canal was humming. Low, broken, a child's humming with half the notes missing, coming up out of the flat water the way the threads came up out of the rain — and it was the *kitchen*, it was the exact kitchen, the stool and the bowl and the two fingers inside the rim, humming for someone in a doorway, and it was coming from the water that the Hollow had poured itself into, and it was *her*, it was the memory of her, unfinished, the feeling it never got to finish, and Ren stood in the rope-sealed rain with his palms open and his whole body one held note, because if he moved, if he made a sound, if he did anything at all to acknowledge that he knew the tune —

"You hear it too," Yuki said. Not a question either. She was looking at him with the slate held against her chest now, the way you hold a thing you're suddenly not sure you want to record. "It's a memory-pattern. Sixth-order. I've only seen one in the flesh once, at the academy, in a jar." A pause. Her jaw again, the figure that didn't fit the column. "The pattern's a child. Female. Six years old, by the resonance." She looked up. "Who is she, Ishikari?"

And Ren Ishikari, who had spent four years learning to stand very still around the hole in his own chest, stood very still, and said, in the flat voice the criers used for official corrections:

"I don't know."

It was even true. That was the worst of it, and the truest, and the thing that would keep him up for the next three hundred and sixty-seven chapters: he did not know. He had paid not to know. The Tide had taken the name and the face and the four years of her, and left him the kitchen and the humming and a hole, and standing at the rope with the rain coming down wrong, Ren understood for the first time what a Tithe actually was.

It was not a price. It was a *wound the world agreed to call a price*, so the world wouldn't have to look at it.

Yuki wrote something on her slate. He saw her hesitate — saw the cadet's hand and the Warden's hand argue about one line — and then she wrote it, whatever it was, and turned the slate away, and said:

"Come to the Ledger hall tomorrow. Ninth bell. There's a page in your file I'm not cleared to read, and you're not cleared to read, and I've decided that's not acceptable." She was already packing the fork, the fish, the slate. The coat was too big for her. The mask hung at her hip, face-down, never yet used. "Don't make me regret the decision, Ishikari. The hall doesn't like listeners."

"The hall doesn't like anyone."

"No," Yuki Ashihara said, and for one line, one breath, the cadet under the coat looked out. "That's what it's *for*."

She left through the rain. The canal hummed until the night bell, and then it stopped, mid-syllable, the way a name stops when the world has decided you've heard enough of it.

Ren stayed until the dark. He did not put his palms down once.
