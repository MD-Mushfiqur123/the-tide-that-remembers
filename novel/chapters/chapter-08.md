# CHAPTER EIGHT — Low Tide

The drowned shrine of the Choir Below could only be reached twice a day, and only by people willing to climb down into a canal.

The flooded canal behind the fish market was the deepest in the third district — the Sump, the debt-rows called it, because everything the district did not look at eventually drained into it — and at low tide the water fell away down old stone stairs that no map had ever admitted to, forty steps into the dark, to a door that had been a cathedral door once, and was underwater again by dusk.

"You understand," Yuki said, at the top of the stairs, lamp in one hand, badge in her pocket, badge *deliberately* in her pocket, "that this is the part where I stop being able to write anything down."

"That's why we're doing it at night."

"That's why *you're* doing it at night. I'm doing it because the paper in your shirt says *see me*, and the only organization in this city that worships the man who wrote it holds its services at the bottom of a canal." She looked down the wet stairs, at the candlelight coming up out of the dark like something breathing. "If anyone asks, I'm auditing a drainage complaint."

"They won't ask. The Choir doesn't ask. It's their whole personality."

The stairs were slick and old, older than the hall, older than the Sundering maybe, and at the bottom the door stood open on a nave that had drowned upright: pews under waterline-marks, a floor of black stone, and candles — hundreds of them, in glass floats, drifting at knee height where the tide had left them — and the light off the water went up into a vaulted dark where the ceiling should have been and was instead a *surface*, the underside of the canal, holding like a roof of grey glass.

And the Choir was singing.

Not a hymn. A *count*. Soft, patient, in unison, grey-robed men and women standing in the flooded nave with candles in their hands, singing numbers — down, and down, and down — and Ren, palms open before he could stop himself, heard the count for what it was and went cold all over.

They were counting the tide. Not tonight's tide. *The* tide — the two-hundred-year tide, the retreat of the sea from the drowned world, measured in verses, sung down one grain at a time since the Sundering. The Choir had been singing the Sundering's *receding* for two hundred years, the way you sing a child down into sleep, and tonight's verse was:

*— and the water falls, and the door stands further from the sky, and the one who knocked is one grain closer —*

The singing stopped. Every grey face turned. Not hostile — *welcoming*, which was worse.

"You brought the paper," said the one who came forward through the shallows. Old, dry, quick-eyed, with the unhurried hands Ren had already learned to fear in ink. "Good. We prayed it would be read. Most sealed pages rot unread. It's the hall's favorite kind of silence."

"You know what it says," Yuki said.

"We know what it *doesn't* say, which is more." The deacon — they called each other by no names, Ren would learn; names were tithes, and the Choir had paid theirs in advance — stopped three paces off, on the dry lip of the nave, and looked at the folded sheet through Ren's shirt as if the paper were transparent. "One sister, whole. Witnessed by a Lantern. Intercepted. And you have come to the people who sing to the kept, little brother, because everyone else in this city told you the word *intercepted* was a clerical error."

"Tell me what it means."

"It means the vault does not hold her." The deacon said it simply, the way you report weather. "Tithes go to the vault. All tithes. The drowned world is the inventory; the Glass is the door; nine keepers hold it shut from inside. That is the shape of the world, and the Wardens' whole umbrella is built on not saying it out loud." A candle was passed, without ceremony, into Ren's hands. "And some tithes — a handful, in two hundred years — never arrived. The hall calls it loss in transit. The liturgy calls them *the kept*. Tithes taken from a life and kept out of the vault, held on the *near* side of the door, by the ninth keeper himself." The quick eyes rested on him, patient, dry. "Your sister is a kept thing, Ren Ishikari. Held where you could find her. Held where she could find *you*. And no one living knows why the Ferryman keeps what he keeps — except the Ferryman — and he does not explain. He only offers. It is his whole personality too."

---

They answered what could be answered. That was the Choir's manner — not lies, not oaths, just the creed, laid out in candlelight like a hand of cards, face up:

The Tide was real, and the Tithes were real, and the price was real — *and the price was not a punishment*. That was the first card. "The Wardens teach that the Tithe is a tax," the deacon said. "A cost of power, paid to the rain. The liturgy teaches otherwise. The Tithe is a *ladder*. Every rung you climb, the Tide takes what holds you to the bottom — and what holds you to the bottom, little brother, is *unfinished grief*. You cannot climb carrying it. The sister, the wanting, the goodbye — these are ballast. The Tide is not cruel. The Tide is *buoyant*."

"And the Hollows?" Yuki's voice, from the shallows, ruled and thin.

"The Tide's orphans. What it takes from you does not die. It waits. And a waited-for thing, kept waiting past its hour, stands up." The deacon shrugged, grey and terrible. "The Wardens open them. The opening scatters them. The liturgy—"

"—finishes them," Ren said. "I've heard. A finished feeling doesn't stand."

The deacon smiled for the first time, and it was the smile of a man hearing his own creed read back to him by a boy with a paper in his shirt and a door under his city. "The listener has been listening."

Then the quick eyes went past them both, up the wet stairs, toward the city, and the smile went out like a candle pinched.

"But hear the last card, and hear it carefully, because the tide is coming and we do not say this verse twice." The deacon's voice dropped to the register of the count. "The kept one walks. She has stopped hiding — you know this; the low places of your district emptied two nights ago; the shame-pool that screened her is certified *cleared*, in the hall's own language, by the hall's own masks. She is in the city. She is looking. And she is looking for warmth, because warmth is what she was cut out of, and you are the warmest thing in Kagemori that smells like her kitchen." A pause. The candles moved on the water. "When she finds you — and she will find you, it is only tide now — do not run."

"Why?" Yuki said.

"Because unfinished things cannot chase, Warden." The deacon looked at her, then at Ren, and the dry old eyes were suddenly, terribly kind. "They can only *arrive*. But if you run — if you teach her that the warmth runs — you will teach her to chase. And a Hollow that has learned to chase is no longer a question. It is an answer. And answers, in this city, get opened."

---

The tide turned while they climbed.

They came up out of the Sump into the flooded canal's rising water, lamp high, and the last verse of the count followed them up the stairs from below, sung soft, sung down, the way you sing a thing to sleep:

*— and the door stands further from the sky, and the kept one walks, and the brother climbs —*

And on the water above the drowned shrine, passing over it as they surfaced, going the same direction they were walking — toward the third district, toward the market, toward the debt-rows and the low places — went a flat black boat with a lit lantern that did not flicker in the rain.

The Ferryman did not look at them. He never did. He only poled, unhurried, patient, two hundred years patient, toward whatever fare was coming due.

"Deacon," Ren said, at the top of the stairs, into the dark below, "one more card. The paper says *see me*. Two words, in his hand. What do I do when I see him?"

The answer came up the stairs after them, out of the candle-dark, out of the count, with the water rising over the fortieth step behind it:

"Ask him what he *refused*, little brother. Not what he took." A pause, and the last of the tide-noise, and then, very softly, the kindest and most frightening thing anyone said to Ren Ishikari in the first arc of this saga:

"Everyone asks the taking. The kept ones are all refusals. Your sister is not a price that was paid. She is a price that was *declined* — and someone, four years ago, in a kitchen, in the rain, declined to let the world have her." The water closed over the stairs. "Find out who. It was not you. You were thirteen, and drowning, and you have spent four years believing you are the one who sold her. But the paper says *intercepted*, and the paper does not lie. The *slate* lies. The paper—"

The tide took the rest.
