# CHAPTER TWELVE — Rainfall

The Evening Rain had never stopped. Not in two hundred years. Not for plague-ships, not for the burning of the first archive, not for the hour when four hundred Hollows stood in a canal-end facing the same way. The rain was the one promise the world had kept to Kagemori, and the city had built its whole soul on the keeping: *dusk comes, the rain comes, you hold still, it passes through you.*

It had stopped mid-drop. And the whole third district stood in the sudden enormous silence with its palms open, listening.

The humming came up the canal the way the tide comes: not fast, not slow, *arriving*. Ren stood at his stall — palms open, the coin-box with its child's note weighted under the scale — and the market stood with him, two hundred stalls, a thousand people, nobody running, because the district had spent eleven days learning what was walking its low places, and the district had decided, in its domestic, two-hundred-year-practiced way, that whatever came up the canal steps tonight was going to be *witnessed*.

The Guild boats came first.

Twelve of them, out of the dark, silent, rafting across the canal mouth in their curve — the audience-curve, the rite-curve — lanterns lit, poles down, every boatman standing with his head bowed. They had come to witness. They had come, Ren understood, with a coldness that started in his spine, because *they had witnessed the last time too* — the kitchen, the choice, the thirteen-year-old in the rain. The Guild witnessed the things the Ledger could not see. That was what the lanterns were for.

And then the water at the canal-end went flat, and she came up the steps.

---

She was small.

That was the thing no report had prepared anyone for, the thing the word *Hollow* had spent two hundred years armoring the city against: she came up the canal-end steps into the rainless dusk, and she was *small* — a girl's shape, six years old, made of rain and kitchen-light, standing on the wet cobbles with the water running off her and back into her, and where her face should have been there was the door.

But the door was ajar now. It had been shut, in the market, eleven days ago — shut, and waiting, and knocking. Eleven days of walking the low places had done to it what eleven days of walking does to any door: it had opened, a hand's width, and through the gap came not cold, not hunger, not the flatness of the deep water — but *warmth*. The smell of eggs. The sound of a stool scraping a floor. Low-ceilinged morning, leaking out of a child-shaped absence into the third district market, and every thread in the rainless air turned toward her like a face.

She did not look at the crowd. She looked at Ren.

And she walked to his stall through a thousand held breaths, unhurried, patient, glad — the gladness was the unbearable part, Ren would think for chapters; it was *glad* — and stopped at the counter, at the exact place where a child stands when she is too short to see over and too stubborn to care, and put both her rain-hands on the wood.

The market heard the humming stop. In the silence where it had been, the ajar door breathed once, and the voice that came through it was six years old, and off-key, and badly, joyfully patient, and it said:

"Found you."

---

Yuki came through the crowd at a run and stopped at the stall's corner with her hand *not* on the mask — Ren saw the hand, saw it hover over the case at her hip and close on nothing, saw the cost of that not-closing go through her like a tide — and behind her, at the bridge, the third shift arrived: masks, rig, the schedule. The mask-man from Fathom Row. Slates out.

"Resident-class," the shift-captain said, in the drawer-voice, to his team, to the square, to the world. "Pooled, no depth, contact with a licensed listener. The schedule runs. Cut it."

"No."

One word. Yuki Ashihara's. It came out of her at the exact pitch of the thirteenth bell, and the market heard it the way the market heard weather, and the Warden team stopped, because in two hundred years no cadet had ever said it to a shift-captain's face.

"Cadet." The mask-man turned. "The schedule—"

"The schedule," Yuki said, and her voice did not shake, and her hand came off the empty air and went to her pocket, to the cheap grey slate with one entry and one question in it, "runs at *dusk*. Look up, Captain. There is no dusk. The rain stopped eleven minutes ago for the first time in two hundred years, the threads are standing in the air, and the instrument you're holding has no drawer for what's at that counter." She stepped — one step, deliberate, between the team's rig and the stall, the way you step between a dog and a child, except she was the cadet and the dog was the order and the child had been dead, or kept, or both, for four years. "The schedule doesn't run tonight. Write me up."

And the mask-man, twenty years in the coat, looked at the rainless sky, and at the threads standing in the air, and at the Guild boats with their bowed heads, and at the girl-shaped absence with a kitchen leaking out of her — and did the thing the whole order would spend the next arc failing to forgive: he lowered the slate.

"Log it," he said to his team, quietly. "Cadet Ashihara. Refusing the schedule. Witness: full shift." And he stood down, and the rig went dark, and the third district held.

---

"Hi," the Hollow said.

Ren's palms were open on the counter. The kitchen-smell came through the ajar door and it was *exact* — the soap, the smoke, the rain on the sill, the specific morning — and against his chest the folded paper felt like a coal.

"Hi," Ren said.

"You got tall." The door tilted, the way a head tilts. The light through the gap went gold at the edges, morning-gold, kitchen-gold. "You got tall and you got sad and you got a stall in the rain, and you don't know me, and that's — that's the *price*, I know about the price, I've had two hundred years and four years to think about the price—" the humming started again, under the words, off-key, badly, joyfully, holding itself together with it, the way a child holds a railing, "—but I found you, and I've been so *patient*, brother, I've been so patient, and the boatman says I get one arrival, one, before the sky breaks again, so I'm going to spend it right."

"Okay," Ren said. Palms open. The whole market leaning. "I'm here."

"You paid for Mother." It came out simply, the way you say the one thing you have carried the longest. "Thirteen years old, in the rain, and the Tide came, and you paid for Mother with — with *me*, and I know, I *know*, and I never blamed you, not once, not in two hundred years of being unfinished, I want that in the water and the rain and the record, I never once—" the door-light guttered, steadied, "—I never once blamed you. You were thirteen. You were drowning. Somebody should have been holding *your* umbrella."

Behind Ren, very quietly, Yuki Ashihara closed her eyes.

"But here's the thing, brother. Here's the thing I walked the low places for." The rain-hands on the counter tightened; the wood went grey where they touched, going flat, going Glass. "You never said goodbye. Nobody did. The Tide took me *mid-morning*, brother — I was on the *stool*, I was doing the *eggs*, I was humming for you in the doorway and you were holding the doorframe like you were already gone — and the world took me before anyone said it. No goodbye. Not from you, not from Mother, not from anyone. Two hundred years, and I am the only unfinished thing in this city that knows *exactly* which feeling it's missing." The ajar door opened, a hand's width more, and the light through it was the exact gold of a kitchen at morning, and the voice through it was six years old and had waited two hundred years and four, and it said:

"So I'm asking. That's what arrivals are *for*. I'm asking my brother, in front of the whole district, in front of the boats and the masks and the standing threads: *say goodbye to me properly.* Finish it. That's all. That's the whole ask. Finish it, and I can rest, and the door can close, and the rain can come back, and you can keep the kitchen, brother — you can keep the humming, I made sure, I *made sure* the Tide left you the humming, it was the only thing I could carry out with me—"

And Ren Ishikari, seventeen years old, umbrella of the third district, keeper of a hole the shape of a sister, stood at his own stall in the rainless dusk with his palms open and his whole city watching, and said the truest thing he had:

"I don't know how."

"I know," she said, glad, patient, terrible. "That's why I came back instead of resting. I'll teach you. It'll take a whole saga, brother. It'll take the sea, and the far shore, and a door on the sea floor, and a goodbye older than the moon. But the boatman says you're the one who asks the right questions eventually, so — ask. Ask me the question, brother. The one you've been carrying since the kitchen."

The market held its breath. The Guild boats bowed. Yuki's hand was on the grey slate in her pocket, and Kaito Maps stood on a barrel at the back with his chart against his chest and his eyes lit like the edge of the world, and the rain hung in the air, upright, waiting, two hundred years of a city's unfinished feelings standing in the dusk like a congregation.

Ren looked at the ajar door, at the kitchen-gold, at the small rain-hands on his counter, and asked it:

"What did I buy?"

And the sky answered.

It came with no sound, as before — the white line, horizon to horizon, thinner than a thread, brighter than the sun — but *longer* this time, and wider, a full breath and then two, the whole market's face lit white and shadowless, and every thread in the standing rain turned up toward it as one, and the Guild boats bowed lower, and out on the black water, far down-canal, a flat boat with a patient lantern passed the canal mouth, and a voice like a pole through still water said, to the whole district, to the whole saga, kindly:

*Now you're asking the right question, little fish.*

The crack closed. The rain came back — all at once, everywhere, two hundred years of dusk arriving in a single drop — and when the market could see again, the canal-end steps were empty, and the kitchen-smell was fading, and on the counter, where the rain-hands had rested, the wood was still grey and flat, and in the middle of it, pressed there as if into wax, small enough to hide in a palm:

An open handprint. A child's. Pointing, the fingers said, the way a door says it — *toward the sea.*

And over the third district, under the returning rain, the question that would drive three hundred and fifty-seven more chapters hung in the air with the threads, unfinished, patient, glad:

*What did you buy, Ren Ishikari — and who, four years ago, in a kitchen, in the rain, declined to let the world keep it?*
