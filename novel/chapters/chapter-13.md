# CHAPTER THIRTEEN — Outrun the Ledger

The write-up on Yuki Ashihara reached command the morning after the rain came back, and the order that came back down reached the third district by noon, and it was two lines long, and everyone in the district who could read a schedule understood that the city had changed.

*CADET ASHIHARA: suspended, pending review. LISTENER ISHIKARI: remanded to the hall for study. Asset class: anomalous.*

*Study.* The word went through the third district like a fish-knife. Kagemori had two hundred years of practice with what the hall did to anomalous things: it opened them, or it indexed them, and either way they did not come back with their interiors.

"Right," said Kaito Maps, at the fishmonger's shed, with his chart against his chest and his stolen compass swinging outside his coat like a struck bell. "Boat's at the Sump stairs. We leave tonight."

"We?" Yuki said.

"There's a *we* now. There's been a we since the bridge. You think I ran six days ahead of the Wardens by being fast? I ran by being *loud*, Warden — loud is a route. The whole eastern market knows a smuggler with a chart came through, and every one of them saw the runners go into the jars, and not one of them will say so in a hall, because the third district has decided." He looked at Ren, and the laugh banked down to the lit place. "You pointed at the sea, brother. The child's handprint. I was there. I saw it. The ask is the sea — she said it herself, in front of God and the whole pickle stall: it'll take the sea, and the far shore, and a door. So we go to sea."

"The Glass takes the crew's memories," Yuki said. "That's not law. That's *physics*. No ship crosses with its mind."

"No ship crosses *slowly*," Kaito said. "The fraying's a tide, not a wall — a day on the Glass takes the shore's smell, two days takes the shore's name, a week takes the shore. My brother's run made the far side in nine days on the wrong heading, and what the Glass left of him was enough to sound the deep water and die happy. We have the right heading. We have the chart. And we have—" he patted the compass, "—a Warden needle, recalibrated, which the Cartographers will tell you is impossible, and which I will tell you is *stolen*, and between impossible and stolen I know which one sails."

Ren looked at the harbor light through the shed's boards. At his stall, out there in the rain, with the child's handprint pressed grey into the counter. At eleven years of dusk practice, two hundred stalls, O-Tama's jars, Suzu's coin-box — the whole warm domestic machine of the district that had put its body between him and the schedule without being asked.

"I can't finish it here," he said slowly. "She said it herself. The goodbye needs the sea." He closed his palms — really closed them — and opened them again, and the deciding was done the way the Fathom Row deciding had been done: not considered. *Arrived.* "Tonight, then."

---

The Lantern Guild moved them out at the black hour, and moved them the way the Guild did everything: without a sound, without an index, without one word wasted.

Twelve boats at the Sump stairs. The market's whole underworld of unindexed men, poling the three of them and Kaito's sea-chests down the flooded canal in the dark, past the drowned shrine, past the low places, out through the harbor's back-door — a channel the charts did not admit to, between the pilings of the old toll-gate, where the water ran glass-flat and the Lanterns' lanterns burned without flickering.

The oldest boatman poled Ren's boat himself.

Ren had seen him for eleven years at the dusk ferries — the still one, the one who never took a fare, the one the other boatmen stepped around. He was older than the hall's oldest clerk and drier than the archive, and his pole made no sound at all, and halfway down the back-channel, in the dark, in the glass-flat water, he spoke for the first time in eleven years of silences.

"I held the lantern," the old man said.

Ren's palms went cold. "When."

"Four years ago. A kitchen. A choice." The pole moved; the water did not argue with it. "You were thirteen, and drowning, and the Tide was taking, and someone has to hold the light when the Tide takes, little fish — that is the whole of the Guild's office. We witness what the Ledger cannot see. I held the lantern, and I saw what was taken, and I saw what was done with it." The old eyes came around, flat as the channel, kind as the archive. "The hall's slate says the Tithe went to the vault. The slate lies. The paper says *intercepted*. The paper is kind. Here is the truth the lantern saw: she never went near the vault at all. We ferried her ourselves — the Guild, that night, under orders older than the hall — to the water's edge, and the water's edge to the boat with the patient lantern, and the boat with the patient lantern took her *out*. Not under. Out." The pole lifted, turned, and pointed — down the back-channel, out through the harbor gate, at the sea. At the Glass. At the flat dead horizon where the sky's crack lived. "She is not in the vault, brother. She is *at large*. And she has been at large for four years, walking your low places, because the man who kept her out wanted her where you could find her — and where she could find you."

"Why?" Ren said. "Why would he—"

"That is not the lantern's question to answer." The old man's pole came down; the boat slid through the harbor gate, out from under the city, into the open dark. "But I will give you the lantern's gift, which is free, like all the best things in this city. You asked the deacon what to do when you see the Ferryman, and the deacon said *ask what he refused*. I will tell you what the Guild has seen, in two hundred years of holding lights over other people's worst hours." The flat old eyes rested on him, and there was — under the dryness, under the two hundred years — something that in an ordinary face would have been called love. "The Ferryman has refused exactly one thing in his whole long office, little fish. He refused to ferry *her*. Every Tithe in this world goes where it is told. Hers, he carried out of the world's hands with his own, and set down where the rain could not reach and the vault could not file and only a brother's ask could ever move her again." The pole swung; the harbor light sank behind. "The hall calls that an interception. The Guild calls it the only mercy the system never indexed. Now — hold the lantern a moment. It's heavy, the first time."

---

Kaito's ship was waiting at the sea-buoy: a smuggler's lugger, black-hulled, low, wrong in all the ways a ship can be wrong and still be loved — and named, in flaking paint on her transom, in a hand that had clearly been laughing when it wrote:

*WRONG WAY.*

"She's named for the only truth I own," Kaito said, catching the line the Guild threw, with the whole of his lit grin. "The wrong way is where the whales sing. You'll learn."

They went aboard in the dark. Yuki stood at the rail with her cheap grey slate in her pocket and her badge — her whole previous life — in her hand, and looked at it for a long moment, at the coat's weight and the oath's weight and the umbrella's weight, and then she did the thing the second Ledger had been building toward since its first entry:

She dropped the badge into the Glass.

It did not sink. The sea took it the way the sea took everything, flatly, completely, without a ripple — and Yuki Ashihara, cadet no longer, Warden no longer, watched the water close over the one identity the hall had ever given her, and said, to the sea, to the slate, to the saga:

"I'm keeping the coat, though. It's warm."

They made sail in the black hour and ran out past the last buoy, and the Glass took them the way it took everything: gently, flatly, without one sound. And behind them, at the harbor mouth, the twelve Lantern boats rafted in their curve and lit — every lantern, all at once, the whole unindexed witness of the city of Kagemori — and held the light until the ship was a dot, because that was the Guild's office: *someone has to hold the light when the Tide takes.*

And down in the hold, in the collected rainwater of the bilge, where no one was listening, the water began — softly, off-key, badly, joyfully glad — to hum.
