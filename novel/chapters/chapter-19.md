# Chapter Nineteen — Yuki's Letter

The sloop came up on the fourth morning out of the Saint's rock, and it did not run them down, and it did not run away, and both of those facts were worse than either alternative.

It was a Warden hull — grey, low, the rain running off it in sheets — and it flew no colors, because Wardens did not need colors; the shape of the bow was the color. Nine crew at the rail, grey coats, and every one of them wearing the white mask, face-forward, which was the regulation for *openings*, and there was nothing to open, and Ren counted the masks twice and got nine both times and did not like the arithmetic at all.

Yuki was at the rail before the sloop finished its approach. She had slept in the chartroom again, on the slate, and the coat — the grey coat, regulation, too big for her, inherited from someone who had died in it — was buttoned wrong, and she looked at the nine masks the way a person looks at a tide-table that has stopped agreeing with the water.

"They're not boarding," Kaito said, glass up. "They're — huh. They're *waiting*. There's a boat coming off."

"There's a boat coming off," Ryudo repeated, from the stern, where he had taken up a post he never explained, facing backward, watching the wake, watching — though only Ren had worked this out, and Ren kept it filed — the sea floor, far below, where the whistling thing walked ahead of them at their own speed, patient as arithmetic.

The small boat came across. One oarsman, one passenger, and the passenger was young — younger than Yuki — and wearing a lieutenant's pins and a grey coat that fit, and carrying a oilcloth packet in both hands like something that might go off.

"Wrong Way," the lieutenant called, when the water between them had shrunk to a boat's length. "Ashihara Yuki. Warden, formerly of the Ninth Ledger, Kagemori hall." A pause, in which the rain did its work. "I'm ordered to say: *formerly* is under review. And to give you this." He held up the packet. "By hand. It was that or the bell, and I've — " the young voice went somewhere honest for a second — "I've read the routing slip, ma'am. Nine of us came out. The sea sent fewer back. I'd rather hand you a letter than find out what the routing slip meant, if it's all the same."

Yuki looked at him for a long moment. Then she reached down and took the packet, and her open hands did not shake, and Ren, watching, thought: *she's learned that from him* — and then thought, *no; she always had it; she just stopped hiding it.*

"Suzu," Yuki said.

The lieutenant went still. "Ma'am?"

"Nothing." Yuki's voice was flat as the Glass. "You remind me of nothing. Go."

---

They read the letter in the chartroom, because the chartroom had a door, and because Kaito refused to let salt air near his chart, and because Ryudo stood outside it facing the wake, and because some documents want walls.

The letter was one page, hall stock, the watermark of the Ninth Ledger pressed into it like a face under cloth. Yuki read it aloud, evenly, the way she read tide-tables, and Ren listened, and the rain listened, and the bilge — the bilge hummed, low, off-key, joyfully, its tune now carrying a harmony that no one had put there, and no one mentioned, and everyone heard.

*"Warden Ashihara. Your badge has been recovered from the Glass at Fathom Row and your file reopened. The hall extends reinstatement: full rank, Ninth Ledger, back-pay to the date of your absence. Conditions follow.*

*One. You will deliver the Ishikari boy to the hall, unharmed, unsampled, at your earliest crossing. The hall is aware he is bound for the Depths. The hall is aware of the ask. The hall has decided the ask is weather.*

*Two. The thirteenth bell has been filed as weather. Do not file it otherwise.*

*Three. W-9 remains what it was. The hall has reviewed the seal's provenance and found the review closed. Some paper is load-bearing, Warden. Do not pull it.*

*Four. Your sister — "*

Yuki stopped reading.

The chartroom was very quiet. The bilge hummed. Outside, the sloop's nine masks watched the hull, and the rain came down on all of it, Evening Rain, patient, the rain that had been falling on this coast since the world was filed.

"I don't have a sister," Yuki said, to the letter, in the voice of someone checking arithmetic. "I have a brother. He's in Kagemori. He's — " and then she stopped, and Ren watched her not say the rest, and understood, in one cold drop, the shape of the thing the hall had just done: it had put a *lie* in the letter, a small one, a cheap one, the kind of lie you put in a net to see what struggles.

Point four went on, in the hall's flat clerk-hand:

*"— your sister has been located and is well. Come home, and the hall will consider the matter closed. There is no fifth condition. There has never been a fifth condition. Burn this letter. — Oribe, Warden-Captain, for the Ninth Ledger."*

"There's more," Kaito said.

There was more. A postscript — one line, and the line was not in the hall's hand. The hall's hand was a stamped thing, square, tired. This hand was *open*. It slanted up the page like something walking uphill on purpose, and Ren knew it before Yuki turned the page to the light, because he had seen it pressed into the wax of a sealed drawer and stamped onto a page that said *Intercepted. See me.*

The open palm. The same hand. The same signature of no name.

*"P.S. — The coat burns, Warden. The mask is yours. It was always yours. — S."*

---

Yuki read it twice. Then she did the thing that Ren would spend a long time understanding, and that the saga would spend longer: she put the letter down on the chart table, weighted it with the slate, went up on deck into the rain, took off the grey coat — the coat that had been doing her worrying for her since she was a cadet, the coat a dead Warden had left her, the coat that fit like an apology — folded it once, properly, the way you fold a flag or a shroud, and gave it to the brazier.

The wool went up grey-green and reluctant. Kaito watched from the chartroom door. Ryudo watched from the stern. The nine masks watched from the sloop, and did not move, and that was the strangest part — nine Wardens, ordered to retrieve a deserter, watching her burn her own uniform and making no move at all, as if the order they were under had a condition nobody had read aloud.

When the coat was gone, Yuki stood in her shirt in the rain, thinner than the coat had ever let anyone see, and unhooked the white mask from where it had hung face-down at her hip since chapter one of her life — never yet used, never yet opened anything — and turned it face-forward, and did not put it on.

"It was always yours," she said, reading the air, or the postscript, or the rain. "From who?"

The sea did not answer. The sea had never once answered Yuki Ashihara directly; it answered her *ledger*, and her ledger was in the chartroom, and the ledger now had two entries and one question, and the question had just grown a postscript.

The sloop put about without a word. Nine masks went away into the rain. The lieutenant, last aboard, looked back once — and Ren, watching, saw the young man's hand come up, briefly, and open, palm out, and close again, and the gesture was so small and so deliberate that it was either nothing or it was everything.

"They'll report the coat burned," Kaito said. "Oribe will read the report and know you've chosen. There'll be more sloops. There'll be worse than sloops."

"There'll be paper," Yuki said. She came down into the chartroom, wet, coatless, mask now hanging face-forward at her hip like an eye finally allowed to open, and she took up her slate, and she wrote, in the second ledger, under *Resolved: no* and under the question that had been standing there since the wax door:

*Entry three. The hall filed my brother as a sister. The hall knows things about my family that my family does not. The hall is not the hall's own customer.*

She looked at the entry a long time. Then, beneath it, in small square letters, she added:

*Entry four. Find who S. is. Find what the mask was, before it was mine.*

And below the hull, in the dark, the whistling thing — which had been walking ahead of them all night, unhurried, patient, glad — stopped, and turned, and for the first time in four hundred miles of sea floor it was not whistling the tune.

It was whistling the *harmony*.

Ren stood at the rail with the Saint's paid grain still warm somewhere behind his ribs, and listened to a kitchen's morning coming up out of the deep in two parts, and understood, with a listener's terrible clarity, that the thing below them knew Yuki's postscript was about it — and was *pleased*.
