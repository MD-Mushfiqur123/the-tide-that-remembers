# CHAPTER TEN — The Tithe

The Warden infirmary kept its patients in the dry, which was the cruelty no one had ever fixed: a room with no rain in it, for people the rain had taken things from.

Ren was three days in it. The hall's physicians — drawer-voiced, thorough, kind in the way schedules are kind — found nothing wrong with him. Pulse: regular. Tide: settled at the Second Depth, stable, textbook. Memory: intact for age, district, trade, family, archive. The hole did not show on any instrument, because the hole was not an absence of anything the body kept. It was an absence of *meaning*, and meaning has no pulse.

On the first day, Yuki came with the rig's recording and played it for him.

He watched himself save the child. That was the strangeness the infirmary had no drawer for: the flood, the doorway, the hundred ropes of light, his own hands going out — all of it on the slate, all of it *him*, and none of it arriving anywhere. He watched the way you watch a stranger's bravery: with respect, at a distance, the way you read a good report.

"That's me," he said, when it ended.

"Yes."

"I don't— " He made himself say it plainly, because she had earned plainness, three nights in a canal and a paper in a shirt ago. "I believe you that it's me. I can't feel that it's me. It's like watching the widow's humming go up. Someone else's unfinished thing."

Yuki turned off the slate. Sat with it on her knees. The infirmary's dryness pressed in, the room where the rain never came, and she looked — for a long time — at the man the Tide had finished emptying, one rung at a time, one kitchen at a time, and she did the thing the manual had never once asked her to do and the second Ledger was going to require every day for the rest of the saga:

She told him a true thing the records couldn't hold.

"Her name is Suzu," Yuki said. "She's eight. Debt-row, low end, Fathom Row. She sells fish-cakes at the market. She has come to this room every day for three days, at the visiting bell, and sat where I'm sitting, and you have asked her, each day, kindly, whether her family has been found." The ruled voice held. Her hands did not. "She was at the bridge when you took the rung, Ishikari. She saw the whole flood. She saw you pull a street's worth of unfinished grief through your own chest and finish it with your bare hands, to keep it off one child. And when it was over, and the Tide had taken the rescue out of you — the *memory of saving her*, root and all, so you'd never once get to feel what you did — she picked up her coin-box, walked out of the floodwater, and went back to her stall. And she has been coming here every day since. Not to be thanked. To bring you a fish-cake." Yuki looked up. "Because you don't remember, and she has decided that someone in this room is going to remember for you."

Ren sat in the dry room with the rainless window and the fish-cake from that morning, untouched, on the table, going cold, and the Ren-shaped hole in him — the old one, the kitchen one, the four-year one — moved against its walls like a thing that had heard its name.

"Why does it matter," he said, slowly, "that I don't remember?"

"Because of the pattern," Yuki said. "Look at the pattern, Ishikari. It's the only thing the hall's instruments can't un-see, and I've been staring at it for three days." She set the slate on the table, live, the hall's auto-entries, his life in the Tide's own hand:

*Aged thirteen. Tithe: one (1) sister, whole.*

*Aged seventeen. Tithe: one (1) rescue, whole.*

"Whole," Yuki said. "Both times. Not the memory of her — *her*. Not the memory of the saving — *the saving*. The Tide doesn't take feelings anymore, not from you. It takes *events*. It takes the thing entire, the meaning, the weight, the reason you'd have gotten up in the morning — and it leaves you the facts, the way you leave a coat on a hook." She was leaning forward now, and the ruled voice had gone somewhere else, the voice from the ferry, the dead slate, the dusk where two people traded truths because neither could afford to keep them. "The first law says the Tide takes what you can least afford. It took your sister when you were thirteen and drowning. Fine. That's the law. But three nights ago you weren't drowning, Ishikari. You were *choosing* — and the Tide stood there and watched you choose, and then it reached into a finished thing — a *finished* thing, the rescue was done, the child was whole, the flood was out — and it took the finish." Her fist closed on the slate's edge. "The Tide doesn't take what you can least afford. The Tide takes what you *most*. There's a difference, and the difference is a word, and the word is *aim*."

The dry room. The cold fish-cake. The rainless window, and two hundred years of rain outside it.

"You're saying someone's aiming it," Ren said.

"I'm saying the Ferryman keeps a girl out of the vault for four years and no one knows why. I'm saying the Lanterns witness what the system can't see. I'm saying a deacon in a drowned church told you your sister was *declined*, not paid, and that the paper says *see me* in a hand that doesn't brown." Yuki stood, coat too big, mask in its case, second Ledger in her pocket with one entry and one question in it. "I'm saying I've read the pre-Ledger archives, and the old liturgies, and the seal registry, and the tide-tables, and every impossible thing in this city points at one sentence, and I am going to write the sentence in the second Ledger tonight, and you are going to hear it before I write it, because you've earned it three times over."

She said it. Standing, in the dry, in the room with no rain:

"The Tide isn't a law, Ishikari. The Tide is a *clerk*. And clerks take instructions."

---

Suzu came at the visiting bell, as she had come every day.

She came in with the fish-cake — warm this time, from the stall, wrapped in paper — and put it on the table by the cold one, and sat where Yuki had sat, and looked at him with the patience of the debt-rows, which is the patience of people who have never once been able to afford haste.

"You don't have to keep coming," Ren said. Gently. The market voice, the umbrella voice. "I'm all right. You don't have to—"

"I know," Suzu said.

She was eight, and she unwrapped her own hands from around the paper, and she looked at the two fish-cakes on the table, warm and cold, and then at him, and said the thing she had been carrying up the bridge-steps every day for three days, the thing the second Ledger would spend two hundred and forty chapters trying to earn:

"You don't remember it. That's the price, isn't it? I heard the Warden lady. The Tide took the rescue so you'd never get to feel it." She shrugged, debt-row quick, eight years old and fluent in the only language the low end had ever taught anyone, which is *what things cost*. "So I thought — somebody has to hold it. The rescue. Somebody has to keep it somewhere, or it's just gone, and that's worse than the flood." She pushed the warm fish-cake an inch toward him, not asking anything, not waiting for anything, a child doing the work the whole city had hired an order of masked strangers to fail at. "So I hold it. Every day I come, and I remember it at you. That's all. You don't have to do anything."

She slid off the chair at the bell, eight and quick and gone, and Ren Ishikari sat in the dry room with two fish-cakes and a hole the shape of a rescue, and did not touch either one, because his hands had gone to his face, and the umbrella of the third district was weeping for the first time in eleven years at a kindness he could not remember earning.

---

That night, the rain over the third district fell gently, first-layer, harmless, a city's small farewells going up like sparks — and down in the low places, in the drained dark where the Sump met the sea-wall, the kept one walked.

She had stopped hiding. She had stopped waiting. She walked the waterline of the third district with a patience that had outlived two hundred years of tide, and she was *glad* — the deep gladness of an unfinished thing that has finally felt its brother climb — and she followed the smell of a kitchen that no longer existed anywhere but in her, through the debt-rows, past the leaning houses, up toward the market, toward the bridge, toward the dry room with the light in the window where her brother sat with his face in his hands, learning to weep at a rescue he would never remember.

She did not hurry. Unfinished things cannot chase.

They can only arrive.

And at the fish market, at the corner of the listener's stall, the coin-box stood in its place with the day's take in it, and on top of the coins, weighted against the wind, in a child's careful hand, was a note the second Ledger would one day file under the only heading that had ever mattered:

*For the umbrella. You held it. I saw. — S.*
