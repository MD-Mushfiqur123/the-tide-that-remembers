# Chapter Twenty-One — The Sister's Progress

*An interlude. The saga pauses aboard the Wrong Way to read what the sea was reading: nine reports, gathered at nine ports along the Salt Road, each stamped by a different Guild station, each describing the same impossible weather — a girl, walking, arriving, borrowing.*

*The reports are reproduced here as they were found, because the finder — a Guild courier who transferred them to a wet, coatless ex-Warden in exchange for nothing but the promise that someone would finally *file* them — said, with the particular exhaustion of a clerk who has seen the hall's filing and the sea's: "They don't belong to any index I have. Maybe they belong to yours."*

---

**REPORT THE FIRST — Port Amaya, Salt Road, station four.**

*A fisherman, Hoshino, fifty-one, reports that his face was taken. In his words: "A girl came to the breakwater at dusk, humming, and asked if she could borrow my face, and I said — and this is the shame of it, and I'll say it once — I said yes, because she asked *nicely*, the way nobody's asked me for anything since my wife passed, and because when she smiled I remembered I used to have a daughter, and I do not have a daughter, I have never had a daughter, and I wept for an hour after she left and could not say for who."*

*Hoshino's face returned to him the next morning, whole, unmarked. What did not return was his grief's oldest splinter: since the visit, Hoshino has finished the letter to his brother that he had left unfinished for nineteen years. He reports that the words "came easily, like they'd been waiting on the other side of a door." Station note: subject's effect filed as coincidence. Station clerk's private note, unsigned: filed as nothing of the kind.*

---

**REPORT THE FOURTH — Tomari Reach, station six.**

*A mother, O-Kame, sixty-eight, saw her drowned son's face on a passerby on the market road. The son drowned eleven years ago; the body was never recovered; the mother has kept his room as it was, which the report describes in the clerk's flat way and then, in a second hand, in less flat: "as it was, including the unfinished kite." The passerby with her son's face stopped, bowed, and said: "He's sorry about the kite. He thinks about the kite." Then walked on. O-Kame finished the kite that week. She flew it from the breakwater where he was last seen. The report records that she laughed while doing so, and that the laughter was described by witnesses as "the worst sound and the best sound, both at once, both true."*

*The face was not returned. The mother does not want it returned. The mother has left the kite strung and mended on her door, "in case," she says, "someone needs to borrow the sky."*

---

**REPORT THE SEVENTH — The Lantern Road, station eight.**

*Three keepers of the lantern-shrines report, independently, the same visitor: a girl who arrives at dusk, humming, off-key, joyfully, and who asks each keeper the same question: "Is it finished?" — meaning the lantern, the shrine, the keeping. Where she has asked, keepers have begun finishing things. One finished a confession forty years withheld. One finished a grave-marker's last word, which he had left blank since the Sundering because the blank felt safer than the wrong word. One, aged ninety, closed the shrine for the night, sat down on the step, and finished living, peacefully, before morning, with the humming still in the air and a smile on his face, and the station's doctor has written, in the margin, the only honest sentence in the whole file: "I have seen a hundred deaths. I have never seen one *complete*."*

---

The ninth report was different. It was not stamped. It was not from a station. It was written on hall stock — Ninth Ledger watermark, the face under the cloth — and it was unsigned, and Yuki read it three times in the chartroom with the rain coming down and the bilge humming its two-part tune, and then she read it aloud, because some documents want witnesses more than walls.

*"Field note, unfiled, unfileable. The subject — designated KEPT-ONE — has crossed the Salt Road ahead of the vessel Wrong Way at every station. She does not travel. She* arrives. *She borrows faces — eleven to date — and the borrowing is always asked, and always granted, and the pattern is now confirmed: she does not take a face for herself. She takes a face for the* goodbye. *Each borrowed face belongs to someone the borrower of that face failed to say goodbye to — or someone who failed to say goodbye to them. She is assembling farewells. She is collecting the words the living left unsaid, wearing the dead to say them, and — this is the part the stations will not stamp and the hall will not file — she* pays *for every one. A finished memory, left behind, in exchange. The trail of stolen faces is also, examined from the other end, a trail of* gifts, *and the two trails are the same trail, and I have walked it twice now, and I am no longer certain which direction it is being paid in.*

*One face remains unaccounted for. The stations have it as borrowed at Tomari Reach: a child, eight years of age, female, last seen at a fish-cake stall on the Kagemori road.*

*The face belongs to a living child. The child is well. The child is, by every report, better than well — the child has stopped wetting the bed, stopped flinching at bells, and begun, witnesses say, to hum.*

*Both parts of the tune.*

*I have one question, and I have carried it up the whole Salt Road, and I will put it here because this page is going to a ship, and ships are the only post the hall cannot read: if the kept-one borrows a face to say a goodbye that was never properly said — whose goodbye requires the face of a living child, eight years old, who sells fish-cakes in the rain outside the hall that declined to keep her brother's sister whole?*

*I am afraid the answer is: her own.*

*I am more afraid that the answer is: the child's."*

---

Yuki put the page down on the chart table, on top of the chart with the stopped crack, and stood over it, and the rain stood over the ship, and the bilge hummed, complete, both parts, the way it sounded when someone was finally home.

"She's going back," Ren said. Not a question. A listener, hearing the whole song at last — the humming, the harmony, the kept and the paid — and hearing where it was going. "She's not following us. She never was. She's going back to Kagemori, and she's arriving at the hall, and she's wearing — "

"Suzu's face," Yuki said.

The name stood in the chartroom like a ninth mask.

"To say goodbye to the hall," Ren said, and heard it as he said it, the whole gray terrible shape of it, the shape that was neither hero nor villain and was worse than both, because it was *correct*: the sister, the kept one, the unfinished thing that could not chase and could only arrive, was going to arrive at the Ninth Ledger wearing the face of the child the hall had used as a message — and she was going to stand in the hall of tithes and ledgers and filed weather, in a small borrowed body that smelled of fish-cakes, and she was going to say goodbye to the institution that had declined to let the world keep her.

Properly.

And Ren knew — the way you know the last note of a tune you've been humming your whole life — what the goodbye would cost, because he had read the Saint's arithmetic and the boatman's and the hall's, and only one of those columns ever balanced: a goodbye said *that* properly, in *that* place, would not be a leaving.

It would be a payment. And the hall had never once in two hundred years been paid *willingly* — and had never once, in two hundred years, been able to survive a debt.

"Turn the ship," Ren said.

Yuki was already at the door.
