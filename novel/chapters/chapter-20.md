# Chapter Twenty — The Ferryman's Fare

The Ferryman came up from the hold on the evening of the same day, and Kaito, who had been guarding his chart the way saints guard relics, looked up from it and said, "No," before the man had said anything at all.

"No?" said the Ferryman.

"Whatever it is," Kaito said. "Whatever you want, whatever you're selling, whatever the fare is. No. I've seen what your passengers pay. I've read her ledger." A nod at the chartroom, where Yuki's slate lay with its two entries and one question and its new small square postscripts. "I'm a mapmaker, not a tithe. I don't have anything you'd take, and I'm not going to find out what you'd take anyway."

The Ferryman considered this. He was doing what he always did — no one aboard had yet caught him arriving or leaving; he was simply *present*, the way weather is present — and his lantern, the one that had no place in the rigging and hung there anyway, was lit, and the light of it did not move when the ship moved.

"I don't want anything," he said.

"Everyone wants something."

"Yes," the Ferryman agreed, mildly. "I want the same thing I've wanted for a hundred years, and it is not for sale, and it is not aboard. What I *have*," and he took something from inside the boatman's coat, something small and wrapped in oilcloth, and set it on the chart table beside the chart with the growing crack, "is a fare that was paid to me, and that I cannot deliver, and that has been riding in my coat for longer than your hall has existed. Undelivered fares are a boatman's shame. I am told — " and something moved at the corner of his mouth, the ghost of an expression that on any other face would have been a smile — "that shame sinks, and longing waits, and grief drifts. This one has been sinking in my pocket for a century. I would like it to arrive."

Kaito did not touch it. Kaito was, in Ren's private taxonomy of the crew, the careful one — the one who counted the masks twice — and he looked at the packet the way he looked at reefs.

"Paid by who?"

"By me," said the Ferryman. "To me. That is the trouble with it. You cannot witness your own payment, and an unwitnessed advance does not hold — she taught me that, the Saint, forty years before she was the Saint, back when she was only a girl on a dock with good arithmetic and a bad feeling." The lantern swayed; the light didn't. "I paid it wrong, and it never took, and it has been in my coat ever since, going nowhere, being a hundred years of weight. Fares want to *arrive*, mapmaker. Even unpaid ones. Even wrong ones. Especially those."

"What is it?" Ren asked, from the doorway, and the Ferryman looked at him — and Ren had been looked at by Hollows and clerks and a sister who wore the whole sky's patience, and none of it was this; this was a man looking at a door he had nailed shut from his own side.

"A memory," the Ferryman said. "Of the other side of the sky."

---

Kaito opened it at midnight, alone, in the chartroom, with the door shut — and this is what the saga knows of what he saw, because Kaito told it once, badly, and never told it again, and the telling is hereby preserved exactly as he gave it, in the galley, three days later, with his hands around a mug of the wrong tea:

"It's a sea," he said. "That's the first thing. Everyone thinks the other side of the sky is — I don't know. Doors. Light. It's a sea. The Glass, but from *underneath*, if you can hold that in your head — you're below it, looking up, and the surface is a sky, and there's weather on the underside of the water, and it rains *up*, and the rain comes down — " his hands did something helpless in the air — "the rain comes down somewhere else. That's what our rain is. That's what the Evening Rain *is*. It's the other side's weather, falling through. We've been living under someone else's sky, and the sky is *sea*, and it has tides, and the tides are the — " he stopped, drank, started again. "The Depths. The Nine Depths are its tides. The Tide isn't a power, it's a *weather*, and the hall has been filing a sea as a tax code for two hundred years, and that's the least of it."

"The least of it," Ren said.

"There's someone walking on it." Kaito's voice went flat, the way Yuki's went flat, the way voices on this ship went flat when the alternative was not flat. "On the underside of the sea that is a sky. Walking, the way you'd walk a beach. And there's a door standing on the surface — no island, no hinge, just a door, upright on the water, and the walker was going toward it, and the walker — " and here Kaito's careful mapmaker's voice did the thing it had clearly been doing every night since, alone, in the dark, over the chart — "the walker turned around. In the memory. A hundred years ago, in a boatman's memory, the walker turned around and *looked at him*, at the memory, at the watching, the way you look when you know you're being remembered — and it waved. Cheerfully. And it had a child's hand."

The galley was very quiet. The bilge, below them all, hummed its tune with its new harmony, and nobody looked at the floor, and everybody heard.

"And the sound," Ren said. "Memories that weigh nothing hold everything. What was the sound?"

Kaito looked at his tea.

"The kitchen-tune," he said. "Whistled. Correctly. Both parts." He turned the mug. "The walker was whistling it, and something on the other side of the door was whistling it back, and it was — " the mapmaker's arithmetic finally failed him, visibly, in the set of his mouth — "it was *happy*. It was the happiest sound I've ever been given by a stranger, and it's been in my head for three days, and I've charted four islands since, and every chart I draw has the door in it somewhere. I keep finding it. Bays. Capes. The curl of a current. I can't stop putting a door on my maps, and if the Guild ever sees my work again they'll strike my name off for it, and I don't care, because — " and he said the last of it very fast, like paying a debt — "because the crack in my chart, the one that's been growing since the thirteenth bell? I measured it this morning. It's not a crack. It's a *coastline*. It's the outline of that door, drawn by a chart that never agreed to be drawn, growing, one hair-width a day, on my table, in the shape of the thing on the other side of the sky."

---

The fare's remainder, the part that was not memory but instruction, the Ferryman delivered at dawn, at the rail, while the Salt Road's next island came up out of the rain ahead of them — a low green place with a drowned bell-tower standing in its harbor, which is its own chapter and its own grief.

"One condition," the Ferryman said to Kaito. "The memory is yours. The knowledge is yours. But the chart — when you chart the crossing, and you will, all of you, sooner than any of us wants — chart it *wrong*. One degree. South of true. My error, and you keep it for me, and you never correct it, and you never tell them why."

Kaito stared at him. "You want me to sail a wrong chart."

"I want you to sail *mine*," said the Ferryman, and for a moment — one moment, the first in the whole saga — the boatman's composure went out like a lantern in wind, and what was under it was not age and was not patience and was not calm; it was a hundred years of a man who had rowed every grief in the world to its door and had never once been allowed through his own. "One degree south of true is where the whales sing, and the whales sing where it's safe, and I have known that for a hundred years and I have never once been able to say it, because a boatman who tells you where the safe water is is no longer a boatman; he's a *guardian*, and guardians get tithed." The composure came back, grain by grain, paid, witnessed, out loud. "You're a mapmaker. Errors are your trade. Keep mine."

Kaito kept it. That morning, at the chart table, with the whole crew watching, he took his pen and drew the new course — one degree south of true, deliberate, witnessed, like a lantern lit in company — and the crack in the chart, the growing coastline of the door, *moved*.

It moved one hair-width. South.

And it stopped growing.

Nobody aboard slept well that night, and nobody aboard could have said whether it was because the crack had stopped, or because it had clearly only stopped *where they could see it* — or because, far below the hull, in the dark, at their speed, the whistling thing walking ahead of them had changed its tune again: not the melody now, not the harmony, but both at once, hummed and whistled together, the whole kitchen's morning, complete, the way it sounded when the door was open and someone was finally home.
