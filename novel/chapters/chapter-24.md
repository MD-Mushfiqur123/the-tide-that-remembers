# Chapter Twenty-Four — The Name

The whistling thing climbed for an hour, and the crew of the Wrong Way stood at the rails and watched the clear water over the drowned city, and did not run, because there was nowhere to run to that was not *down*, and because — Ren understood this, and understood that the others understood it, and that nobody said it — you do not run from the thing that has been walking ahead of you since the thirteenth bell. You have been *following* it. That is what ahead means. It was never pursuit. It was escort, and the escort had finally been paid enough to come up.

It came up at the kitchen.

Not at the hull. At the kitchen — at the hill of small houses, at the lit window, at the drowned street outside the door that had opened and stayed open — and it came up the way a man comes up a garden path at the end of a hundred years of road: slowly, deliberately, letting himself be seen. The water bent around him. The lantern-light of the finished city lay on him and did not go through him, and that was how they knew he was not a Hollow and not a ghost and not any filed thing at all: he had *edges*; he took up the world; he was, whatever else he was, a man.

A man, gaunt as a mast, in the ruin of a boatman's coat, with rope-scarred hands and a face weathered past all reading — and one arm ended at the wrist in a child's hand, small and whole and pink and *new*, a hand that did not match the century of the rest of him, a hand that swung at his side like a lantern that had chosen him — and he stood in the drowned street outside the kitchen door, and he looked up, through the clear water, through the hull, directly at Ren.

And he whistled. Both parts. The whole kitchen's morning. And he waited, at the door, like a man who has walked four hundred miles to stand exactly there.

"Let him in," Ren said. His voice did not sound like his voice. "He's paid."

---

They went down at slack water, the four of them, in the ship's bell-caps, into the drowned city that the index called a wreck — down through water clear as glass, past lanterns that had finished and stayed, up the hill of small houses, along a street where the awnings were still folded for the night two centuries long — to the kitchen with the three set places and the steaming bowls and the door that stood open, and the boatman-thing stood aside from the door to let them pass, and bowed, boatman-deep, the bow of a man who has ferried every grief in the world and knows the exact weight of this one, and Ren, going in, touched the man's rope-scarred wrist — the grown wrist, not the child's hand — and the man closed his eyes like a debt acknowledged.

Inside, the kitchen was warm.

That was the second unbearable thing, after the steam: it was *warm*, two hundred years under the sea, and the lantern on the table burned without consuming, and the three bowls steamed, and the height-marks climbed the doorframe — *and the name-board on the wall was no longer paid.*

The letters had come back.

They stood on the painted board in a child's careful brush-hand, four characters, whole, restored — returned with the tide, paid back with the sky's interest, come home with the grains like everything else that had been carried away — and Ren looked at them and the kitchen went silent around him, because the letters were in *his* alphabet, in his family's alphabet, in the hand his mother wrote the market lists in, and the name they spelled was —

The saga does not write it. Not here. Not yet. Understand this as a *rule of the world* and not a dodge of the author: in the old world, names were Tithes, and a name restored is not yet *paid for*, and a name read aloud before its debt is settled does not light a lantern; it *opens a door*, and the door it opens is the one under the hall, and there are three bowls on the table and only two of them are for the family. The name stood on the board, whole, restored, unpaid — and Ren read it, silently, with his listener's eyes, and his hands opened all the way, and he did not say it, and the kitchen held its breath around him, and *approved*.

"She's here," Yuki said.

She was. In the doorway to the pantry, where the light was thinnest, wearing a face Ren had never seen and knew instantly: a young woman's face, plain and tired and kind, with flour at the jaw — a borrowed face, asked for and granted, the face of some drowned great-great-grandmother out of the height-marks' earliest years — and she was wearing it the way you wear your mother's coat: too big, and exactly right. The sister. The kept-one. Ren's sister, kept a hundred years by a boatman's palm and a declined form, standing in the first kitchen, in the oldest kitchen, in the kitchen the tune was born in, wearing a dead woman's face so that she could have a face for this.

"You read it," she said. Not a question. Her voice was the humming, given words. "You read it and you didn't say it. You *paid attention* instead." She smiled — the borrowed face smiled, tired and kind and flour-jawed, and behind the smile something hundred-years-old and patient let itself, for one moment, be glad. "Good. Listen, then, brother, because the tide is turning twice tonight, and I have only the slack water between the turns, and the boatman — " she nodded past them, at the doorway, at the man with the child's hand, who stood with his eyes still closed — "the boatman has walked the longest, and he waits on my word, and my word is almost ready."

She came to the table. She looked at the three bowls. She turned the middle one, a half-turn, the way you turn a cup for a guest.

"The hall has my name in the vault," she said. "It went into the vault the night I was declined — that is what a decline *is*, brother; the world is not refused you, the world is *invoiced* — and a name in the vault is a name the world cannot call. That is why your letters could not hold it. That is why the Lantern Island's oldest lantern went dark when I passed. That is why the registry line beside your seal is blank in the same hand as my board was blank: we are *family paper*, you and I, filed in the same vault, and the vault is under the hall, and the hall is ringing its thirteenth bell tonight — they ring it every year, did you know, on the anniversary of the Sundering, to *celebrate* that the lid held — and I am going to arrive there tonight, wearing the small face of a girl who sells fish-cakes, and stand in the hall of tithes, and say goodbye to the institution that invoiced me." The borrowed face did not change; the kitchen got colder. "But a goodbye said to a nameless thing does not *count*. The hall taught me that. Nothing counts there that is not filed. So the name must be said — my name, at the door, by *family* — and a name can only be said by someone who has paid for it, and the price of a name, brother, in the old world, in *our* world, was always the same."

She looked at his open hands. The child's hand in the doorway, at the boatman's side, opened too.

"The price is a name of equal weight," she said, gently, terribly, kindly. "And yours is the last one in the family that was never tithed."
