# CHAPTER SEVEN — The Sealed Page

The Warden archive lived under the Ledger hall, and the Ledger hall lived over the hinge, and no one in Kagemori except the Lantern Guild knew which of those facts was the load-bearing one.

Ren knew the way down because there was only one kind of person the archive forgot to guard: listeners. The guards at the sub-level door checked coats, masks, slates, and hands — palms open was a felony below ground — and they never checked whether you could *hear*, because in two hundred years no one had thought to make listening a crime you could commit by accident.

So it was not a break-in, exactly. It was Yuki Ashihara, third shift, badge legitimate, walking a documented district listener down the archive stairs on a witness-addendum pretext with her heartbeat going like a fish in a bottle, and the door-guards nodding at her badge and never once looking at Ren's palms, which were closed, and stayed closed, the whole way down, which was the hardest discipline he had ever practiced, because the archive was *loud*.

---

Paper, below the hall. Actual paper — the pre-slate originals, two hundred years of it, filed in long dark aisles by district and year, and the paper leaked the way old things leak: thin, constant, a white noise of first-layer grief, the archive breathing out the shed feelings of every entry it had ever recorded. Ren walked the aisles with his palms closed and the noise pressing on him like deep water, and understood, in the soles of his feet, why the hall was built on top of this.

The dead weight of a city's paid grief makes excellent ballast.

"Third district. Year of the low tide." Yuki's lamp swung. "Originals are filed by *payment date*, not entry date — the hall re-indexed the slates twice, but the paper never moved. If your Tithe was witnessed, there'll be an original with the witness mark." She pulled a drawer. The wood was swollen with two hundred years of rain-air. "Ishikari. Once we open this, it's real. The seal on your slate is a *seal* — above my rank, above the Marshal, maybe above the hall. But paper predates wax. Whatever they sealed, they had to seal *after* writing it." Her lamp steadied. "I want you to be sure."

"I've been sure since the canal hummed."

"Wrong answer. Nobody's sure since the canal hummed. That's why I'm asking."

Ren looked at the drawer — third district, the year he was thirteen, the year of the low tide, the year of the kitchen — and made himself say it properly, the way you say a thing you will be held to:

"I need to know what I bought. Whatever it costs to know."

"That," Yuki Ashihara said, pulling the drawer the rest of the way open, "is the first honest sentence I've heard in this building."

---

The file was thin.

That was the first wrongness. Two hundred years of archive, and the Ishikari file for the year of the low tide held one sheet: the original entry, the hall's ruled hand, older ink, the wax-stamp of the district office gone brown at the edges. Yuki laid it flat under the lamp with the tenderness of someone handling a wound, and Ren read it standing up, palms closed, and the archive's white noise went quiet around him, the way a room goes quiet when something large passes underneath.

*ISHIKARI, REN. Aged thirteen. Authority: self, distressed. Witnessed: yes.*

*Tithe: one (1) sister, whole.*

Whole. Not the memory of a sister. Not the name. The *sister* — an asset class the manual did not contain, a line item the hall had no drawer for, a thing the Tide was not supposed to be able to take, written down in the hall's own ruled hand by a clerk who had written it and, judging by the pressure of the pen, pressed hard enough to go through to the desk.

And below the entry, in the witness line, where a name should be:

A mark. Not a signature — a *stamp*, small, grey, worn: a lantern.

"The Guild," Yuki said. Very quietly. Her lamp-hand was not steady. "The witness was a Lantern boatman. Ishikari — the Guild can't witness. They're not indexed, they're not licensed, they're not *in* the system. A witnessing has to be hall-recorded to be valid, and a hall-recording requires an index entry, and they don't have—" she stopped. Started again, in the voice of someone re-ruled three times in one night. "This entry is impossible three ways. The asset class is impossible. The witness is impossible. And it's *here*. Filed. Valid. The hall accepted it." She looked up. "The hall doesn't accept impossible things. The hall is the thing that *defines* what's possible. So either the rules are wrong—"

"—or someone made the rules lie," Ren said.

The archive breathed its white noise. Two hundred years of paid grief, shelved and orderly, and somewhere in it a file one sheet long that the whole system had agreed, for four years, not to see.

"There's more," Yuki said. "Look at the bottom."

Below the witness mark, below the brown stamp, in the margin, in a hand that was not the hall's ruled hand — a hand quick, dry, unhurried, the hand of someone who had all the time in the world — two words:

*Intercepted. See me.*

No seal. No signature. No date. Just the two words, in ink that had not browned, ink that looked, under the lamp, like it had been set down that morning — and beside them, small, at the corner of the sheet, pressed lightly enough that the paper barely dented: a shape Ren had seen once before, in flat water, in the rain, between one blink and the next.

An open palm.

---

They stood in the aisle for a long time, the lamp between them, the sheet on the drawer's edge, and the archive's noise came slowly back in around them like water into a footprint.

"Say it out loud," Yuki said. "The whole thing. I want to hear how it sounds in a sentence."

Ren made himself do it. Palms closed, in the dark, in the noise: "When I was thirteen, my mother was dying. I took the Tide to save her. The Tide took a Tithe — and the Tithe it took was my sister. Not her memory. *Her.* Whole. And a Lantern boatman witnessed it, and the hall filed it, and someone — the Ferryman — intercepted her before she reached wherever tithes go. And I have spent four years not knowing any of that, and the hall's slate says sealed, and the paper says *see me*." A breath. "And the humming in the canal was my sister, hiding in the city's shame, four streets from my market, for four years."

"Yes," Yuki said. "And here's the part I can't put on any slate, Ishikari, so I'm going to say it once, in the dark, where the hall can't hear its own archive disagreeing with it." The lamp swung, and her face in it was sixteen again, the gallery, the notebook, *I will hold the umbrella so high*. "The Tide takes what you can least afford. That's the first law — the law under the Ledger, the law the whole city is built on. It took you a *person*. People aren't on the schedule. People aren't *in* the system. The system's whole mercy — the umbrella, the drawers, the eleven million entries — is built on the idea that the Tide takes feelings and the city pays them *on purpose*." Her fist closed, slowly, around nothing. "So either the first law is a lie the whole world lives under. Or four years ago, in a kitchen, in the rain, the Tide was *used* — by someone who knew how to make it take a person — and everyone from the district clerk to the seal above the Marshal has been filing the lie ever since."

The knocking started then.

Far below the paper aisles — below the archive, below the foundations, down where the drawers ended and the ballast began and the hinge kept its secret — a sound came up through two hundred years of shelved grief like a knock coming up through a table: patient, unhurried, spaced like a man with all the time in the world.

One. Two. Three.

The archive's white noise stopped. Every aisle, every drawer, every shed feeling: still. And in the stillness, faint, from the direction of the stairs, from the hall above, from the twelve-belled tower over the city — the thirteenth bell began, very softly, to ring.

Not thirteen strokes. One. Held. The way you hold a note when you are listening for whether anyone knocks back.

Yuki looked at Ren. Ren looked at the sheet on the drawer's edge, at the two words in the unhurried hand, at the small pressed palm beside them, and heard, under the bell, under the knock, rising up through the archive like a thread through torn cloth — thin, off-key, badly, joyfully patient, four streets and two hundred feet of shelved grief away:

The humming. *Don't find me,* it had said, from behind a door. But doors that guard departures and doors that wait to be found are different doors, and this one — this one, under the thirteenth bell, under the knock, had changed its tune.

It was humming the way you hum when you have stopped hiding.

It was humming the way you hum when you have decided it is time to be found — and the person humming had chosen, out of all the drawers and all the aisles and all the city in the world, to be found by the brother who paid for her.

"Ishikari," Yuki said, and her lamp-hand was steady now, steadier than it had been all night, because some kinds of fear and some kinds of purpose are made of the same metal. "Tell me that's the pool."

"That's not the pool," Ren said. "The pool's gone. I drained it myself, by standing there." He closed his palms — really closed them, the whole way, the first time since the kitchen — and picked up the sheet, the original, the one sheet, the paper that predated the seal, and folded it, and put it inside his shirt, against the Ren-shaped hole. "That's my sister. And she's done waiting for me to remember her."

The thirteenth bell faded. The knock did not.

"Then we have until it stops," Yuki Ashihara said, and took the stairs two at a time, up toward the hall of eleven million drawers, with a cadet's badge, a dead man's coat, and a second Ledger with one entry in it — *resolved: no* — going up into the rain to unsay, if the world would let her, the oldest lie in Kagemori:

*That what the Tide takes stays taken.*
