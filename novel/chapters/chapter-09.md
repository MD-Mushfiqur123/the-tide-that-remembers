# CHAPTER NINE — Hollow Rain

The rain fell upward on Fathom Row for eleven minutes, and in those eleven minutes a street remembered everything it had spent two hundred years not looking at.

It began, as the bad things in the third district began, at the low end — the debt-rows, the Sump's own doorstep, where the houses leaned together like conspirators and the canal came closest to the doors. Ren was at the market when the pitch changed. He knew it the way the boatmen knew it: the dusk rain thinning, then *stopping*, then starting again wrong — drops rising off the cobbles of Fathom Row like sparks off a struck flint, climbing, unhurried, through the lantern-light, going *up*.

Not grief-drift. Grief-drift was hair-thin, gone in a breath. This was a street's worth, a *generation's* worth, rising in ropes.

"Off the row," Ren said, and the market heard him, because the market had spent eleven years learning what his voice sounded like when it wasn't a market voice. "Off the row, off the low end, up to the bridge — *now*."

They went. Kagemori was two hundred years practiced at leaving low places. And that was why, when the Hollow stood up out of Fathom Row, it found the street almost empty, and the almost was everything.

---

It did not stand the way the market Hollow had stood — a shape, a door, a wrongness in the rain. It stood the way a flood stands. The row's leaked memories had pooled in the Sump for two hundred years — every unpaid debt, every door closed gently in a face, every *I'll pay you next week* that died unspoken — and the pool had been stirred, two nights ago, by the opening of the shame-screen down-canal, and the street's whole sunken past had come loose at once, and now it rose.

It rose as water. The Sump came up the steps of Fathom Row like a tide with an opinion, and where it touched, the row *unfolded*: doors opened on rooms the owners had not entered in years; letters fell out of walls; a man in a doorway dropped his lamp and stood weeping at a debt he had honestly forgotten, weeping with relief, weeping with everything at once — and the flood read him as it passed, gently, terribly, the way you read a letter you have waited two hundred years for.

It was not hunting. That was the horror the Wardens' masks were built for and never once solved. The flood was *reading*. It had been made of unfinished things, and it went through the street finishing nothing, opening everything, a question the length of a neighborhood, asking and asking and asking.

And the Warden team arrived, and made it worse.

---

"Open it," the shift-captain said — not Yuki; Yuki was third district, and this was a third-district night, but the shift rotation had brought a captain from the fourth, a mask-man, twenty years in the coat, and his slate was already out. "Resident-class, pooled, no depth. Standard cut. Cadet, rig the perimeter."

"It's a *street*," Yuki said. "Captain, the pool is a row's whole ledger — if we cut it here, it scatters into the crowd, it roots on every family on this row at once, you'll have forty return-threads, you'll have—"

"Cadet." The mask-man's voice had the drawer-sound in it. "The schedule is running. Cut it."

And Yuki Ashihara, second Ledger in her coat, one entry old, stood in the rain on Fathom Row with her hand on a mask that had never once made anything better, and did not cut it, and the pause lasted three seconds, and in those three seconds Ren Ishikari saw the girl.

---

She was eight. Debt-row born, debt-row quick, one of the bridge kids who sold boiled fish-cakes to the market crowds and left, every dusk, one unasked-for cake on the corner of the listener's stall — *for the umbrella*, she said, because the district had decided that was what Ren was, the umbrella, the man who stood in the rain so the threads rooted on him and not on you. Her name was Suzu, and she had come back for her coin-box.

The coin-box was under the flood.

She was in the doorway of the leaning house at the row's low end, up to her knees in the rising read, and the Hollow's attention had found her the way water finds a drain — not malice; *thirst*. An unfinished thing, and a short life, all of it unfinished: eight years of a child's feelings, none of them done, every one of them wide open, and the flood unfolded her like a letter.

Ren saw it from the bridge. Saw her memories leave her — visible, grey, first one thread and then a handful and then a *hundred*, an eight-year-old's whole short self coming up out of her in ropes of light, her mother's face going, the fish-cake mornings going, her own name going fuzzy at the edges, the child swaying in the doorway with her hands empty and her eyes going flat, going canal-flat, going Glass-flat—

The Tide spoke to him.

It did not use words. It used the shape of the offer, the same shape it had used in a kitchen four years ago, the same shape it uses in every kitchen, every rain, every low place: *you are standing next to a thing you cannot afford to lose, and you are deeper than you have ever been, and the rung is right there.*

Second Depth. Swimming. *Draw a thread out of another's mind.*

He took it. There was no decision in it — decision is for things you consider, and this was not a consideration; this was a kitchen, again, always a kitchen — and the rung took him the way dusk takes a street: all at once, from the edges in.

He went *into* the flood.

Not his body. His hands went out — open, the listener's shape, the umbrella's shape — and he took hold of the hundred ropes of light coming up out of the child, and *pulled*, the way the Ferryman poled: unhurried, patient, with all the strength of a thing that had never once needed to hurry because it was already where everything went.

The flood turned.

The whole street's read came off Fathom Row and onto him — two hundred years of unpaid debt and unsaid things pouring through his open palms, the row's whole sunken past trying to finish itself in his chest — and he held, and held, and *read it for them*, the listener's oldest trick, the thing the stall-keepers paid for without knowing: he took the unfinished feelings into himself and finished them, one by one, fast, the way you finish a sentence for someone dying — *the debt was paid, the door was loved, the week came, they knew, they knew, they knew* — and the flood, finished, lost its question.

It went out like a lamp. The Sump fell back down its steps. The rain on Fathom Row, for the first time in eleven minutes, fell *down*.

And the child was still standing in the doorway.

Whole. Flat-eyed no longer — blinking, swaying, clutching a coin-box to her chest with both hands, eight years old and entirely herself, every thread back where it lived, not one feeling missing, not one morning gone.

Ren let go of the Tide.

And the Tide, buoyant, patient, two hundred years practiced at the taking, collected.

---

He came back up in pieces, on his knees in the wet cobbles of Fathom Row, with the rain coming down ordinary and grey, and the first thing he felt was the hole.

Not the Ren-shaped hole. A new one. Fresh-edged, recent, the specific shape of a thing that had been in him twenty seconds ago — and he sat in the rain doing what listeners do with holes, which is *inventory*, running his palms over his own past like a man patting his pockets, and everything was there, the kitchen, the market, the archive, the paper in his shirt, the deacon's cards, the flood —

The flood.

He remembered the flood. He remembered the row unfolding, the mask-man's schedule, Yuki's three seconds. He remembered *deciding*.

He did not remember what he had decided *for*.

There was a girl in the doorway of the leaning house. He could see her — everyone could see her; the whole street was coming out onto its steps now, the way streets do after floods, quietly, touching their own faces. She was eight, and soaked, and holding a coin-box, and she was looking at him across the wet cobbles with an expression he had seen a thousand times on the faces of people whose threads he had read and never once had words for.

She was crying. And she was saying his name. And the name meant nothing.

Not nothing — *less*. It arrived in him the way a coin arrives in a drained fountain: the sound of it, the shape of it, and nothing underneath. *Ren.* This child knew his name and was saying it like a rope thrown to a drowning man, and he looked at her, politely, kindly, the way you look at a stranger's child in trouble, and heard himself say, in the listener's gentlest market voice:

"It's all right. You're safe. Did you lose your family in the flood? Someone will help you find them."

And the girl stopped crying all at once, the way a tap stops, and looked at him for a long, long moment — eight years old, coin-box to her chest, an umbrella with no memory of holding her up — and said, in the small flat voice of a child who has just learned a word she will spend the rest of the saga unable to put down:

"You don't remember."

"I'm sorry," said Ren Ishikari, who had paid, again, and did not know what he had paid, "do I know you?"

---

The Tide's entry wrote itself into the hall's records before the flood-water had finished draining, and Yuki Ashihara read it by lamplight at the bridge, her hands steady and her face the archive's face:

*ISHIKARI, REN. Aged seventeen. Authority: self, unforced. Witnessed: full row. Tithe: one (1) rescue, whole.*

*One rescue, whole.*

Not the memory of the flood. Not the fear, not the effort, not the pain. The *rescue* — the whole event, the meaning of it, the thing that would have made an umbrella out of him — taken, complete, the way you take a tooth: root and all, so the wound closes clean, so the missing fits its hole so well you can only find it by the cold that comes through.

The first law, the law under the Ledger: *the Tide takes what you can least afford to lose.*

And the Tide, buoyant, patient, learning the shape of Ren Ishikari the way water learns a bed, had looked at a boy who had already lost a sister to the taking, and chosen — with what Yuki could only describe, in the second Ledger, in the small hand that had learned to shake, as *malice, or mercy, or the flatness of deep water* — the one thing that would teach him what he was:

It took the rescue. It left him the child.

*Resolved: no,* she wrote. And underneath, the first question the second Ledger ever asked instead of recorded: *What does it mean, that the Tide has begun taking whole events?*

And down in the low places of the third district, in the dark, in the rain, in the drained and empty low end where a shame-pool used to screen her, the humming that had been patient for four years — the humming that had said *don't find me* behind a door, the humming that had said *brother, stay* under a bell — changed one last time.

It was not hiding. It was not waiting. It was not asking.

It was glad.

Her brother had climbed a rung. The Tide had taken from him again. And the kept one, the unfinished one, the girl-shaped absence four streets away in the dark with a departure and a flood behind her — she felt the rung take him, the way you feel a door close somewhere in a house you know the shape of, and she turned, and she began, unhurried, patient, with all the time in two hundred years, to walk toward the smell of the kitchen.
