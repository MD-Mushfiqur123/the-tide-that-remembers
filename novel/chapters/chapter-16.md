# CHAPTER SIXTEEN — The Mapmakers

The Cartographers of the Glass kept their academy on the last rock before the deep water — a lighthouse the size of a cathedral, burning night and day for sixty years, aimed not at the sea but *up*, at the sky.

"You'll want to know why the light points up," said the night-watch, meeting them at the cliff-stairs: a woman of fifty, salt-cured, with the eyes of someone who had measured the same impossible thing every solstice for thirty years. "It's because we're not a lighthouse. We're a *telescope*. The whole tower is a lens. And the thing we've been looking at for sixty years—" she looked at Kaito, at the chart on his chest, at the four of them standing in the wind with a swordsman's planted sword and a cadet's grey slate, "—you brought it with you, didn't you. The growing line. Sixty years of my predecessors' suicides-in-advance, rolled up in a smuggler's coat."

"The chart came home," Kaito said. "I told you it would."

"Come up, then. And walk fast. The sky's been *off* since three nights past, and the fellows on the top floor haven't slept, and I want witnesses who aren't us when the Warden's seal-registry gets mentioned."

---

The academy was loud. That was Ren's first reading, palms open on the spiral stairs — louder than the archive, louder than the market: sixty years of measured dread, shelved in ledgers, leaking thin and constant the way the lighthouse's light leaked up the stairs. A building that had been *afraid* for two generations, politely, in ink.

The top floor was a dome of glass under the great lens, and the dome's walls were the secret, and the secret was a wall of solstice drawings.

Sixty years. Sixty drawings of the same horizon, one per year, each with the crack measured in a ruled hand — and the wall told the story the chart had only summarized: the line, thin as hair in the early years, patient, linear, a mile a year, *a mile a year, a mile a year* — and then, at the wall's end, the last three drawings, fresh ink:

The line, thicker. The line, thicker. The line — the newest, dated three nights past, the night of the thirteenth bell — drawn not in the ruled hand but pressed, scored, the vellum dented, with a single annotation in the night-watch woman's writing:

*Growth: sixty years' worth. In one bell. The curve has broken.*

"The Sundering is reopening," the night-watch said, to the dome, to the wind, to the four of them. "Not drifting. *Accelerating.* And before you ask who knows: the hall knows. The hall has known for sixty years, because we reported it, three times, through channels, and this is what the channels did with the reporting."

She led them down one floor, to the common room, and the common room was warm, and full of light, and in the best chair by the fire sat an old man who was *smiling*.

He was the academy's finest — his portrait hung over the fire, his ledgers filled a shelf, his name was on the lens's dedication plate — and he sat in his chair by the fire and smiled at the weather and at the tea and at everyone, completely, beatifically, and there was nothing behind his eyes but the smiling.

"Master Hara," the night-watch said, gently, the way you speak of weather that has taken something from you. "Our finest. Forty years ago he found the acceleration. He reported it. The hall's response—" she drew a paper from her coat, an old order, seal intact, and read it in the drawer-voice: "*By schedule, Tithe: one (1) voice, whole. Asset to be maintained in comfort.*" She put the paper away. "He wasn't punished. He was *kept*. They tithe the message and maintain the messenger, Warden — that's the hall's whole art. He sits by the fire. He smiles. He is the most comfortable man on this island. And every solstice, we draw the crack, and we do not report it, and we chart it instead, and the chart goes into a smuggler's coat, because paper doesn't get tithed."

"How many?" Yuki said. Her voice was the archive's voice, and her hand was on the grey slate, and she was writing, writing, writing. "How many announcers?"

"Three that we know of. Hara. And two others, other decades, other channels. All maintained. All smiling. There's a room upstairs we call the *smiling floor*." The night-watch looked at Yuki then — at the badge-less coat, at the slate, at the second Ledger — with the first hope Ren had seen on her salt-cured face. "You dropped your badge in the Glass, they told me at the jetty. Good. Badges get tithed. Lists don't."

---

It was Ren who found the logbook.

He found it the way he found everything now: palms open, in the old vault-room where the academy kept its founding records — and the book was the founding record, the first Cartographer's own hand, two hundred years old, open on a stand under glass, and the room's leak was *quiet*, finished, the leak of a thing written down so completely it had nothing left to shed.

The first page. The Sundering, recorded in real time, by the woman who had invented the science of impossible seas:

*Day of the white line. The sky did not break. I want that in the record, against every account that will follow: the sky did not break, because a thing that breaks has edges, and this had none. The sky* opened. *As a door opens — flat, hinged, total — and for the length of one held breath the world could see through it, and what we saw through it was—*

The ink stopped there. Not faded: *stopped*, mid-line, and below it, in the same hand, decades later, a single appended line, smaller:

*I never wrote what we saw through it. The Tide took it that same dusk, unasked, whole. I have spent fifty years trying to remember a thing I never survived remembering. The one detail the Tide let me keep, I give to whoever finds this book, because it is the only thing I know for certain:*

*Something came through. It was carrying a lantern.*

Ren stood in the quiet vault-room with his palms open and the paper against his chest and the whole saga's ground tilting under him — *the Ferryman came through the Sundering; the Ferryman came from the other side of the sky; the Ferryman is not a man who climbed; the Ferryman is a thing that arrived, carrying a lantern, two hundred years ago, and has been ferrying fares ever since* — and behind him, at the vault door, the night-watch said:

"You've gone white as the line, listener."

"The first Cartographer," Ren said. "She wrote what she saw. And the Tide took it — *unasked*. The Tide doesn't take unasked. The Tide is a clerk, and clerks take instructions." He turned, and the vault's quiet leak moved around him like water around a piling. "Someone silenced the only witness of the Sundering before the ink dried. Sixty years before they silenced Hara. The hall's been *editing the sky*, from the first page."

---

They left the academy on the morning tide, and the academy gave them what it had: the wall's sixty drawings copied in facsimile, the night-watch's own sky-log, and one instrument — the long-scope's eyepiece, the *sky-glass*, wrapped in oilcloth, with the night-watch's blessing: "Point it at the line, if you ever get close enough. And listener — when you see it — remember it was a *door*. Whatever else it is, it was a door."

And as the Wrong Way cleared the cliff-shadow, the lighthouse's great beam swept the sky one last time, up at the thin white thing on the horizon of heaven — and the beam, sixty years steady, *leaned*, the way the Lantern Island's lights had leaned, the way wheat leans, the way everything on the Salt Road leaned now, all together, toward the sea, toward the crack, toward whatever was being asked.

Ryudo stood at the stern with his planted sword and his recovered grief, and watched the light lean, and said, to the horizon, in the voice of a man two hundred years out of practice with hope:

"Everyone on this road keeps looking at that line like it's a wound." He slung the sword. "It's not a wound. It's a *seam*. And seams are where you open a thing — or where you close it."

Down in the bilge, glad and patient, the humming kept time.
