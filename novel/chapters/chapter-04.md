# CHAPTER FOUR — Threads

They met on the lantern-ferry because it was the only place in the third district where a Warden and an unlicensed listener could stand side by side without anyone's slate recording it.

The ferries ran the canals at dusk, lanterns fore and aft, and they were the city's oldest truce: everyone rode, no one reported, because the ferry was where Kagemori went to feel its feelings in company. The boatmen — the Lantern Guild, grey and silent, poling their flat black boats through the leaky dusk — were the only people in the city the Ledger had never indexed. Two hundred years, and no drawer in the hall had a Lantern mark on it. Ren had spent years not thinking about that. He was starting to think about it now.

Yuki sat across from him in the stern, out of uniform, in a coat that was only a coat, and the city went by them at water-level: bridges, laundry, the lit windows of the debt-rows, and over all of it the Evening Rain coming down in the lantern-light like slow glass.

"Teach me," she said.

"Teach you what?"

"What you see." She had her slate on her knees, face-down — turned off, Ren realized, actually turned off, a Warden cadet with a dead slate in her lap, which in the third district was somewhere between sacrilege and a confession. "I've had the academy's version. Layer-one grief, drift-rate, thread-density per thousand. I can pass the exam on threads. I can't *see* them, and the exam doesn't care, and I've decided I do." The ferry rocked. The lanterns swung. "You stood in front of a Hollow with your hands open. I want to know what the world looks like to the hands."

So Ren told her. And because it was dusk, and the ferry, and the dead slate, he told her true.

---

"Everyone thinks threads are memory," he said. "They're not. Memory stays put. What leaks is the *unfinished* — the feeling that never got to be finished. Grief you didn't get to cry out. A thanks you didn't say in time. The widow in the market — she wasn't leaking her husband. She was leaking the *humming*. The part she never got to hear enough of."

The lanterns swung. Yuki didn't write anything. Her hands were folded on the dead slate, and Ren, who read people for a living, noted that she held them the way he held his palms: open, and braced.

"There are three layers," he said. "You'll have the academy's names for them. Here are the ferry's."

"Go on."

"First layer: grief. It drifts. It goes *up* — that's why the rain is lightest over the rooftops and heaviest over the water. Grief wants to leave. It's the only feeling that's trying to." A bridge went by, and under it, in the boat's wake, the water shivered with small rising lights, threads thin as hair, a whole city's worth of small unfinished farewells going up. "A city with only the first layer would be sad and fine. That's most of Kagemori, most evenings. The stall-keepers pay me to stand there because a listener's open palms calm the drift — the threads root on feeling, and if there's a listener near, they root on *him* instead of on the crowd. It's not magic. It's more like… being the tallest tree."

"And the second layer."

"Shame." Ren watched the water. "It doesn't drift. It *sinks*. Grief goes up and shame goes down, and it pools in the low places — canal-ends, debt-rows, anywhere the city keeps what it doesn't look at. Pool enough of it, in one low place, long enough—" he nodded back, the way they had come, toward the sealed canal-end, "—and it stands up."

"The Hollow."

"The Hollow. And here's the thing the manual gets wrong, cadet. The manual says Hollows are litter. Discarded. Nothing." The lanterns swung. "Nothing doesn't *wait*. That thing in the market had me, whatever it wanted of me, four breaths from taking it, and it waited. Litter doesn't wait. A question waits. A Hollow is a question the city asked and never stayed for the answer to, and it goes through the world looking for someone who can finish the sentence it was cut out of."

Yuki was very still. "Third layer," she said.

And Ren looked at her, and made the decision he would spend the rest of the saga paying for, in one direction or the other, and told her the thing listeners told no one.

"Longing," he said. "It doesn't drift and it doesn't sink. It *waits*. It stays exactly where it was made — a doorway, a kitchen, a lane — and it holds its shape, and it hums, and if you come close enough, it doesn't take from you." His palms, open on his knees, the lantern-light on them. "It *asks* you."

The ferry poled on. Under the bridge ahead, the water shivered with rising grief-threads, first layer, harmless, a city's small farewells going up like sparks off a fire that would burn for two hundred more years.

"Sixth-order child's pattern," Yuki said quietly. "In the canal. Waiting."

"Yes."

"Yours?"

"I don't know," Ren said, and it was even true, and they both let it be true, because it was the only true thing either of them could afford at that water-level, at that dusk, with the third layer humming somewhere behind them in a sealed square.

---

Then it was her turn, though she never said it was her turn; she just started talking, the way people do on ferries, at dusk, with the slate off, when someone has gone first.

"Three things the Wardens do," she said. "You'll know the first two. We open Hollows — that's the mask, that's the oath, that's the coat. And we keep the Ledger." She looked up at the lit windows of the debt-rows going by. "The pre-Ledger archives are open to cadets. I read them at sixteen, and I couldn't sleep for a month. Do you know what Kagemori was like before the Ledger? There's a district — you're standing on its fill, the canal district, the ground under the third district is the district — one afternoon, eighty years after the Sundering, a plague-ship came up the canal with its dead, and the district *remembered them all at once*. Every mother who'd lost a child to that ship, every debt unpaid, every goodbye unsaid — no Ledger, no schedule, no umbrella. Four hundred Hollows in one hour, Ishikari. In one *hour*. The old paintings of it — the canal-end full of them, standing in the water, all facing the same way, all waiting —" she stopped, and re-ruled her voice. "The Ledger isn't a tax. It's an umbrella. You pay a little, on a schedule, in a hall, witnessed, and the city never has to remember everything at once. That's the mercy. I've *read* the alternative."

"And the third thing," Ren said. "The thing you're not saying."

The lanterns swung. Yuki Ashihara looked at him for a long moment — the weighing look, the tithable-material look, except turned on herself, and finding the material was already spent.

"Collection," she said. "Quota Tithes. When the Ledger's schedule runs short — when the city's paid grief doesn't cover what the schedule needs — the order collects the difference. From debt-rows. From the unlicensed. From people who can't refuse." The ferry rocked. Her hands stayed open on the dead slate. "It's in the budget. It's been in the budget for sixty years. It's called *schedule maintenance*, and I've signed the forms for it, and the forms are in my coat, and I brought the coat because I keep almost throwing it in the canal."

The boat went under the last bridge before the mooring. The lantern-light came through the slats and laid bars across her face, and Ren, palms open, thirteen years a listener, did the thing he did instead of comforting people: he read her.

One thread. Thin, grey, second-layer, sinking as she sat there — shame, pooled, going down — and he should have let it sink, it was hers, it was none of his, and he read it anyway, because he was seventeen and she had just handed him the third thing:

*A courtroom, small, official. A mother in the dock. A verdict read aloud in the ruled voice of the law, and the word that keeps the whole memory together is not guilty, is not sentence, is not the mother's name: the word is consentless. Paid without consent, says the law, and the mother does not cry, and the girl in the gallery — sixteen, top of her intake, grey coat already ordered — writes in her notebook, in a hand that has not yet learned to shake: I will hold the umbrella. I will hold it so high that no one under it ever gets wet like this again.*

The thread snapped. Sank. Was gone.

Yuki was looking out at the water, at the debt-rows, at the lit windows of the city she had joined to protect, and she did not know she had leaked, and Ren did not tell her — not that night; the telling would take the whole saga — and the ferry touched the mooring, and the boatman put down his pole, and the Evening Rain went on falling on the third district like a city breathing in a sleep it had agreed on.

"Tomorrow," Yuki said, at the rope-ladder, coat on, slate live again, cadet again, "the canal-end. I'm requisitioning an opening team. The Hollow's resident in the water, and the schedule says resident-Hollows get opened within the week." A pause, at the top of the ladder, not looking at him. "The manual says open. The manual's been wrong before. Ishikari — if there's a way to finish one instead—" she didn't finish it. She went up the ladder into the rain, and the coat was too big for her, and the mask hung at her hip, face-down, never yet used.

Ren stayed on the ferry to the end of the line, and back.

And at the third bell of the night — the last bell, the all's-well bell, the bell that said *the city held, the umbrella held, sleep* — the night bell of Kagemori rang thirteen times.

Thirteen. The bell that did not exist. The bell that had not rung in two hundred years, not since the Sundering, the bell the old boatmen called the *knock* — and on the water, all down the canal, every lantern-ferry put its pole down, every light on every bridge went still, and the whole third district listened to the thirteenth bell ring out over the debt-rows and die away into the rain.

The door-warden's words, in the hall of eleven million drawers: *a door shut from the other side is a door that can be knocked on from it.*

Ren stood up in the empty ferry so fast the lanterns swung wild, because he knew — the way you know a room you have never entered, the way you know a tune — that of all the doors in all the world that the thirteenth bell could be knocking on, it was knocking on his.

And from the canal-end, four streets away, through the rain, thin and off-key and badly, joyfully patient:

The humming. But this time, for the first time in four years, there were words under it. A child's voice, six years old, half a syllable from finished, singing to someone in a doorway:

*"Brother, stay."*
