# CHAPTER FIFTEEN — The Duel Island

The Duel Island's rule was posted at its only dock, on a board two hundred years weathered, in letters carved deep enough to survive anything short of the Sundering:

*ALL GRUDGES TITHED AT THE TIDE-POOL. NO EXCEPTIONS. THE SWORDSMAN EXCEPTED.*

"Excepted," Yuki read, at the rail, as the Wrong Way came in past the breakwater. "One man on an entire island of people who pay their anger to the sea — and he's excepted. That's either the safest place in the world or the most dangerous, and I've been a Warden long enough to know it's both."

The island of Kenzaki was, in fact, the politest place any of them had ever landed. That was the joke the Salt Road told about it, and the joke was true: a whole island that tithed *grudges* — carried its anger to the tide-pool at the本的 center of town and paid it into the water, grudge by grudge, debt by slight, until the population went about in a permanent, unnerving, sunlit serenity. Shopkeepers blessed you. Neighbors apologized *to* you. The fish-monger gave Ren the good eel and refused payment with a smile that had no teeth in it anywhere, and Kaito — who had planned to fence half the island by Thursday — walked out of the market an hour later with all his money and a strange expression.

"I hate it here," he said, wonderingly. "I've never been so *unneeded*."

And then, at the tide-pool, in the island's green center, they found the exception.

He was old. That was the first thing — old the way the archive was old, dry as the Lampwright's ledgers, sitting seiza at the pool's edge in a faded coat with a sword across his knees, and he had clearly been sitting there for some time: the grass was worn to soil in a man-shaped circle around him, two hundred years of a man-shaped circle, and his eyes were closed, and his face was the face of a man listening for something that never came.

"Travelers," he said, without opening his eyes. "Welcome to Kenzaki. Before you enjoy our hospitality, you should know the custom. I am Ryudo. I am two hundred and eleven years old. I am the strongest swordsman alive, and I have been sitting at this pool since before your hall was built, and every traveler who lands on this island fights me."

"Why?" Yuki said.

"Because I ask them to." He opened his eyes then, and they were the flat of deep water, and the politeness of the island stopped at them like weather at a wall. "You see, I have a problem, and the problem is the exception. Long ago — before your grandparents, before their grandparents — I was wronged so completely that the whole island came to the pool with me to see it paid. And I swore, over the water, an oath of vengeance. And then — because Kenzaki tithes its grudges, because that is our way, because I was young and the grief was too big to hold — I paid it." His hands, resting on the sword, did not tighten; that was the terrible part; they were at rest. "I paid the grudge into the Tide. And the Tide took it. And the Tide—"

He stopped. The pool moved. Somewhere out on the Glass, very far off, a whale sang.

"—the Tide took the *name*," Ryudo said, "and left me the *oath*. I do not know who I swore to kill. I do not know what was done to me, or to whom, or why the whole island stood witness. I know only the shape of it — the weight of a sworn thing, unfinished, two hundred years in my hands — and an oath without a target is the most patient hunger in the world. So I sit at the pool, and every traveler who lands, I fight — gently, gently, I have never once killed a guest, ask your Cartographers — because every fight is a question, and the question is the only one I have left."

He rose, then — two hundred and eleven years old, rising like a door — and drew the sword, and the sword made no sound either, and the politeness of Kenzaki leaned away from the pool like wheat.

"Are you the one?"

---

They fought him one at a time, because he asked them to, and because — Ren understood, watching, palms open at the pool's edge — the fighting *was* the asking. Each duel was a thread held out: *read me, finish me, tell me if it's you.*

Kaito went first, all flourish and stolen compass, and lasted eleven seconds, and came up laughing with his shirt split and his eyes lit. "Two hundred years of practice! Brother, he didn't even *stand up* for the first five!" Yuki went second, coat off, mask left on the ship, and fought the way she did everything — ruled, precise, reading him while losing — and lasted forty, and stood afterward in the pool's flat light with her fists closed, saying, quietly, to no one: "He's not fighting to win. He's fighting to *be read*."

Ren went last.

He did not fight. That was the thing the island's serenity had taught him in one morning: he walked onto the grass-circle with his palms open — the listener's shape, the umbrella's shape, the shape the Tide had been drafting him into since a kitchen four years ago — and said, "I won't duel you, Ryudo-san. But I'll do something better. It's dusk."

And the Evening Rain came down — the island's dusk rain, thin, first-layer, harmless — and Ryudo the Unfinished, two hundred years at the pool, two hundred years of paid and unforgotten grief, *leaked*.

The thread came off him the way it came off the widow in Chapter One: pale, small, rising, unfolding for anyone near with open palms — and Ren read it, and the reading went through him like the crack's light, and what it held was:

*A night, two hundred years ago, rain, a harbor — a Lantern boat, a collection — a girl of twelve taken off a jetty by men with a hall's papers and a hall's masks, a consentless Tithe, quota-scheduled, and a swordsman coming down the hill too late, and the boat going out flat and black and patient, and the swordsman kneeling in the rain on the jetty with the whole island behind him, swearing, over the water, into the dark where the boat went: a name, a vengeance, a killing oath — and the name of the man on the boat's stern, the name the Tide would take and the oath would keep—*

And the thread's name was a door. The ink would not hold it. The name came to the edge of the reading and *refused*, the way the sister's name had refused, the way the crack refused the charts — and Ren stood in the rain with his palms full of an unfinished vengeance and the name burning a hole in the middle of it, and he could not read it, and he did not need to.

Because he knew the boat.

*Flat. Black. Patient. A lantern at the bow that did not flicker. A tall figure at the stern, unhurried, two hundred years patient — collecting, in the early years, before the office calcified into legend — a quota of consentless tithes, taken off jetties in the rain, ferried out flat and black to the water's edge.*

The Ferryman had not always refused. Once — before the refusal, before the kept ones, before whatever happened in whatever kitchen had turned a collector into a keeper — the Ferryman had *collected*.

And Ryudo's daughter had been cargo.

---

Ren told him. All of it, out loud, at the pool's edge, at dusk, with the whole polite island standing in a silent ring around the grass-circle — the shopkeepers, the eel-monger, the tide-pool's flat water — because unfinished things deserved witnesses, and this island had taught him that much in one morning.

"The man you swore to kill," Ren said, "ferries for the dead sea now. He's called the Ferryman. He stopped collecting — I don't know when, I don't know why; the Choir says he *refused*, one thing, once — but the night he took your daughter, he was the collector. And your oath—"

"Is still true," Ryudo said.

His eyes were open. He had not moved — seiza, sword across his knees, the man-shaped circle of two hundred years around him — and his face was the flat of deep water, and then, slowly, like a crack taking light, it was not. It was an old man's face, and it was weeping, without sound, without permission, the way the island had taught no one to weep because the island paid its grief away — and Ren understood, in the rain, with his palms open, what two hundred years at a tide-pool actually was:

Not patience. *Insurance.* A man who could not remember his grief, sitting daily at the exact place where he had made it, in case anyone ever came who could read the water.

"You could have her back," Ren said. "The daughter. The Tithe. The kept ones — the Ferryman keeps some tithes out of the vault; my sister is one; there's a whole liturgy; the deacon sang it to me in a drowned church. If he kept mine, maybe he kept others. Maybe he kept *hers*."

The sword moved for the first time in two hundred years.

It did not come up. It went *down* — into the grass, point-first, standing, sheathed by gravity and surrender, a planted thing, done — and Ryudo the Unfinished bowed to Ren Ishikari from his seat, the full bow, the one the island gave its dead, and said:

"I have been asking *are you the one* for two hundred years, to every soul that landed. You are not the one. You are the first one who knew *where the one is*." He rose, and took up the planted sword, and slung it — slung it, like cargo, like a tool, like a thing with a destination now — and looked out past the pool, past the polite rooftops, at the flat dead sea and the sky's thin crack above it. "I'm coming with you. I'll fight your watch. I'll bait your lines. And when we find the boat with the patient lantern—" the flat eyes came back, and there was something in them now that had not been there in two hundred years, something with a *direction*, "—you will introduce me to the man who ferried my daughter. And then, boy, you and I are going to have a conversation about *refusals*."

---

They sailed on the midnight tide. The polite island stood at its breakwater and blessed them, every shopkeeper, every neighbor, the eel-monger waving the good eel — and the tide-pool, at the island's green center, lay flat and full, receiving the town's daily grudges, and did not once ripple.

At the rail, as Kenzaki's lights sank, Ryudo stood beside Ren and said, to the horizon, in the voice of a man who had just been given back his grief and found it *lighter* than the not-knowing:

"Two hundred years I thought the Tide was a law. You're telling me it's a clerk."

"I'm telling you what a Marshal's cadet worked out in a dry room," Ren said. "And what a drowned deacon sang. And what a lantern-holder admitted in a black channel." He looked at his open palms — at the umbrella, at the ask, at the whole impossible direction of his life since a kitchen. "Clerks take instructions, Ryudo-san. The question the whole road is going to turn on is: *whose?*"

Out on the black water, far off, a whale sang. And down in the bilge, glad and patient, the humming kept time.
