# CHAPTER ELEVEN — Maps

The man who fell into the third district market at dusk, eleven days after the flood on Fathom Row, came over the bridge-wall backwards, at speed, with three Warden runners behind him and a rolled chart under one arm, and he was laughing.

That was the first thing Ren noted, palms open at his stall, dusk rain coming down: nobody laughed *into* the third district. People laughed in it, cautiously, the way you speak in a library. This man laughed the way weather arrives.

"Umbrella!" the man bellowed, sliding the last three meters of bridge-cobble on his heels with the economy of a man who had practiced this exact entrance on at least four bridges. "You're the umbrella! The listener! The one the threads root on! Perfect — perfect — don't move —" He reached the stall, took Ren's face in both hands with the genial authority of a physician, and looked into his eyes. "Good. Sad. Hollow-ish. Excellent. Now: hide this in your head."

The chart hit Ren's open palms like a cat being rehomed.

"Who—"

"No time. Yes time. Some time." The man spun, coat flaring — a smuggler's coat, salt-burnt, pockets wrong, a Warden compass hanging outside it on a chain like a trophy or a warning — and faced the runners coming off the bridge. "Gentlemen! Evening! Lovely rain! Have you considered the ferry?"

What happened next took eleven seconds and taught Ren more about the third district's soul than eleven years of listening to it. The runners came off the bridge at a charge; the market — the *whole market*, forty stalls, two hundred years of dusk practice — turned, as one, its back. Not dramatically. *Domestically*. A fishmonger stepped his barrels into the aisle. A fabric-seller shook out a bolt of grey. The pickle stall — O-Tama's stall, sixty and laughing and none the wiser — extended a crate of jars into the lane with the blind inevitability of tide, and the three Warden runners of the third shift, who had all been born within a mile of these cobbles, went down into jars, barrels, and bolts, and the man with the chart was over the stall counter and crouched in Ren's awning-shadow before the first of them found his feet.

"You see?" the man said warmly, from the shadow. "The umbrella. The whole district's your coat. I *knew* the intel was good."

"Who are you?" Ren said.

"Maps," said the man, with his whole chest, as if it were a lineage. "Kaito Maps. Smuggler, cartographer, dreamer, nuisance. And you—" he turned in the shadow, and the laugh banked itself down to a fire's worth, and Ren saw his eyes for the first time: not mad. *Lit*. The eyes of a man who had looked at the edge of the world and found it, offensively, *unfinished*. "You're the one who took Second Depth on Fathom Row and finished a street."

The dusk rain came down. At the bridge, the runners regrouped, blew whistles, and were quietly absorbed by the fabric-seller's third bolt. And Ren, holding a chart he had not asked for, said the thing the whole market was thinking:

"How does a smuggler know that?"

"Because I've spent six years buying impossible things," Kaito said, "and two weeks ago, in a flooded canal, somebody's thread went up over the rooftops like a signal fire, and every listener in the eastern districts woke up at midnight with the taste of a kitchen in their mouths. Including," he added, patting the chart, "the Cartographers' night-watch. Who do *not* sleep, and do not forget, and have been watching you, umbrella, the way you watch a crack in a wall."

---

They read the chart in the back of the fishmonger's shed, by storm-lantern, with Yuki Ashiara standing in the doorway in her coat with her arms crossed and her badge in her pocket, because she had been chasing the smuggler for six days and had decided, at the bridge, that the chart outranked the arrest.

The chart was — wrong. That was Ren's first reading, palms open, the listener's reading: the thing was *loud*, louder than paper had a right to be, dense with sixty years of observations in a hand that changed four times down its length, and under all of it, like a drumbeat, one line drawn over and over and over until the vellum had gone silver with it:

The coastline of the Sea of Glass.

"No ship crosses," Yuki said, from the doorway. "That's not law. That's *physics*. The Glass takes the crew's memories; nothing that enters comes back with a mind; the Cartographers gave up charting it before my grandmother was born. This is—"

"Sixty years of illegal sounding-ships, Warden," Kaito said, cheerfully, "crewed by volunteers who knew the price, funded by nobody, dreamed by me. Every line is a run someone sailed until the Glass took them. My brother drew the first one. He's in the Glass now, or his memories are, and this—" his finger came down on the silver line, and the laugh went out of him all at once, like a shutter, "—is what they died finding out."

The chart's edge. Where the Glass met the sky. And there, drawn in the newest hand, in ink still sharp:

A line. Thin. White. Horizon to horizon — *not* on the sea. Above it. In the sky-margin where charts drew nothing, where cartographers since the first map had left the sky blank because the sky was not the sea's to chart: a crack. Dated. Measured. Sixty years of dates down the margin, sixty years of widths — and the widths were not the same.

"It grows," Yuki said. Her voice had gone archive-flat. "The crack in the sky. It's been growing for sixty years."

"One mile a year," Kaito said. "The Cartographers measured it from the shore with the long-scopes, every solstice, for sixty years, and every solstice it's a mile wider, and every time they reported it, the report came back sealed, and the third man who insisted was tithed into smiling in a public square, and after that they stopped reporting and started *charting*, because paper doesn't get sealed, Warden, paper gets *smuggled*—" and his finger moved down the margin, date by date, and stopped. "Except."

The last observation. The newest ink. Two dates, eleven days apart — and between them, the width had jumped. Not a mile. Not a season's grain. The line on the chart, at its newest date, was drawn twice as thick as any entry in sixty years, and beside it, in the night-watch hand, small and pressed hard enough to score the vellum:

*Anomalous growth. Night of the thirteenth bell. Cause: unknown.*

The shed was quiet. The storm-lantern hissed. Outside, the dusk rain came down on the third district, first-layer, harmless, a city's small farewells going up.

"Eleven days ago," Yuki said slowly, "was the Hollow in the market. The thirteenth bell. The flood on Fathom Row. The—" and she stopped, and Ren watched her stop, watched her reach the same arithmetic he was reaching, and arrive together, at the same impossible sum.

*The night you climbed,* Kaito said — not asked; said, the way you say a thing you have been carrying up a bridge-wall. "The night you took your rung, umbrella, the sky over the Glass grew sixty years' worth of widening in one bell. And I have spent eleven days running from the Wardens and two weeks running from the Cartographers and my whole *life* running toward the edge of the world—" the lit eyes came up, and there was the laugh again, banked, burning, absurd, magnificent, "—to ask the only man in Kagemori the sky flinched for: *what did you do?*"

Ren looked down at the chart in his open palms. At the silver line. At the crack, dated, growing, sixty years patient. At the newest entry — *the night of the thirteenth bell* — and felt, against his chest, through his shirt, the folded paper with its two words in the unhurried hand, and heard, as if from very far out on a dead sea, a deacon's voice singing the count down:

*— and the door stands further from the sky —*

"I took a rung," Ren said. "The Tide offered, and I took it, and it took a rescue out of me, and I don't remember the rescue." He looked up. "And eleven days ago, in the low places of this district, something stopped hiding and started walking. And the sky grew." A breath. "I don't know what I did, Maps. But I know who's been keeping score."

"Who?"

"The rain," said Ren Ishikari, and outside the shed, as if it had heard itself summoned, the Evening Rain stopped — mid-dusk, mid-drop, eleven years of never missing — and every thread over the third district stood still in the air, waiting, the whole city's unfinished feelings hanging upright between heaven and the cobbles like a held breath, until, from down-canal, flat and patient and glad, came the humming.

Kaito looked at the rainless sky. Yuki looked at her dead slate. Ren looked at his open palms.

"She's here," he said.
