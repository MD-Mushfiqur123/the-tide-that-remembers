# CHAPTER FIVE — The Lantern-Ferry

Ren ran four streets through the thirteenth bell, and the bell outran him.

That was the wrongness of it. Bells end. Sound spreads, thins, dies — that is what sound is *for*. The thirteenth bell did not thin. It rang thirteen times and then it did not stop so much as *arrive*, hanging over the third district like weather, and every lantern it passed lit a half-second before the sound reached it, as if the light were not answering the bell but *leading* it.

He came around the fishmonger's corner into the canal-end square with his lungs full of rain and stopped so fast his heels skidded.

The rope seal was down. Not cut, not broken — *lowered*, coiled neatly on the wet cobbles, the Warden tape rolled and weighted under a stone, the way you put a thing away when it is no longer needed. And the canal-end, the flat dead water where a Hollow had poured itself and a child's humming lived — the canal-end was full of boats.

Lantern-ferries. The black flat boats of the Lantern Guild, a dozen of them, rafted together across the mouth of the canal-end in a silent curve, lanterns fore and aft, every pole down, every boatman standing. They were not fishing. They were not ferrying. They were *facing* — all twelve boats pointed inward at the sealed water like an audience, and the boatmen stood at their poles the way a congregation stands at the moment before the first word of the rite.

And down the center of the canal, through the gap in the rafted curve, came one ferry that had no boatman.

Ren knew the shape of it before he knew what he was seeing, the way you know a room you have never entered. A flat black hull. A lantern at the bow with a flame that did not gutter in the rain — that did not flicker at all, that burned with the patience of a thing that had never once needed to hurry. And at the stern, tall, upright, hands at rest on a pole that moved through the water without a sound — no wake, no ripple, the canal parting around the hull the way a crowd parts, politely, for something it has already agreed to.

The Ferryman.

The humming had stopped. That was the first thing Ren's body understood, before his mind caught up — the child's tune that had been singing under the thirteenth bell, *brother, stay*, was silent, and the silence had a direction, and the direction was the boat.

The ferry came to the edge of the rafted Guild curve and the curve opened, every boat drawing back a boat-length in perfect silence, and the Ferryman brought his craft alongside the canal-end steps, below the fishmonger's awning, below the place where Ren stood with his palms open and his breath going wrong.

Up close, the Ferryman was — ordinary. That was the horror of him, the thing no painting and no story and no Warden file had ever gotten right. He was tall, and grey, and his face was a face like a face, the kind of face you would forget in the time it took to turn away, and his eyes were the only part of him that was not ordinary: they were the flat of the canal-end water, and the flat of the Glass, and the flat of a slate with a sealed entry, all at once, and they were *patient*. Two hundred years patient. The patience of a man who has never once needed to hurry because everything, in the end, comes to the water.

"You rang," the Ferryman said.

His voice was the voice from the rain. Lantern-boat pole through still water. Ren's palms went cold.

"I didn't ring anything."

"The bell." The Ferryman turned his head, unhurried, toward the canal-end. The flat water did not reflect him. "Thirteen strokes. That is not the city's bell, little fish. The city has twelve. The thirteenth is mine. It rings when a door is knocked on from the far side." He looked back, and the flat eyes rested on Ren with the gentleness of the very tired. "And someone has been knocking on yours for four years."

The rain came down. The Guild boats stood. Somewhere behind Ren's ribs, the Ren-shaped hole turned over in its sleep.

"You're the one the Choir worships," Ren said. His voice came out steadier than he had any right to. "The Ninth Depth. The Ferryman. Two hundred years of tithes."

"I am the one the Choir *misunderstands*," the Ferryman said. "Which is the usual fate of boatmen." He stepped — no, he did not step; the boat simply was closer, the way dusk is closer — and extended one hand, palm up, an open palm, in the rain. "I run a ferry, little fish. One fare, one crossing. I have come to the third district tonight because a fare is due, and I am never late for a fare."

"I don't have anything to pay with."

"Everyone says that." The flat eyes almost — almost — moved, the way still water moves when something passes under it. "You paid four years ago. Thirteen years old, a kitchen, a choice. The hall has it filed. The hall calls it an asset class." The open palm stayed where it was, rain running off it, off it and *through* it, Ren realized; the drops did not land, they arrived. "I have come to offer you the crossing you already bought. Passage. One fare, prepaid. You may bring nothing."

"Where to?"

"Where all paid tithes wait to be finished," the Ferryman said. "You would call it under. The Glass. The vault. The door." The pole moved, a half-stroke, a courtesy. "Your sister is on the manifest."

The world went very quiet, the way the canal-end was quiet, the way a held note is quiet.

Ren stood at the top of the canal-end steps in the thirteenth bell's weather with his palms open and his whole life one held breath, and he did the hardest thing he would do in the first forty-eight chapters of this saga, harder than facing the Hollow, harder than the archive, harder than all the Depths to come:

He said no.

"No," Ren said. "I don't take passages from boatmen who won't say where they're going. And I don't cross water to find my sister on a *manifest*. She's not cargo. She's—" and there his voice failed, because he did not know what she was; he had paid not to know what she was; and the Ferryman watched him fail with something that in an ordinary face would have been called sorrow.

"So the water says," the Ferryman said softly. "Four years, and the grief still unfinished. That is why the door knocks, little fish. Unfinished things knock." He withdrew the open palm. The lantern at the bow did not flicker. "Then I will keep your fare. I keep all fares; it is the whole of my office. And I will leave you the only gift I give for free, the one I gave the sky."

"Which is?"

"A question." The Ferryman took up his pole, and the boat began — without moving, without wake, without one sound — to be further away. "You already paid, little fish. Ask me what you bought."

"Then tell me what I bought!"

"You asked the wrong man." The flat eyes, going, going, the lantern a small patient star between the Guild boats. "Ask the door."

And the canal-end was empty. Not the boats — the Guild remained, twelve black hulls, silent, heads bowed, poles down — but the ferry was gone the way the crack had gone, between one blink and the next, and the flat water lay under the rain like a slate with the entry wiped.

---

He heard her boots before he saw her.

Yuki Ashihara came down the fishmonger's alley at a run — full kit, grey coat, mask at her hip, slate live and *raised*, and Ren watched her take in the scene the way Wardens took in scenes: the lowered rope, the rafted boats, the flat water, him, in one ruled sweep.

"Ishikari." She came up short at the steps, breathing hard, and her eyes went past him to the Guild boats, and stayed there, and Ren watched a cadet's education happen in real time. "That was— the reports call him— there's no file. I checked the index from the bridge. There is *no file*. Not sealed. Not blank. Absent. Eleven million drawers and the hall doesn't have a drawer for—" she stopped herself. Re-ruled. "The Guild neither. Twelve boats, and the index has nothing. Who are they?"

"Ask them," Ren said, "and they'll pole away. They've never been indexed. Not in two hundred years."

"That's not possible. Everything's indexed. That's the *point* of—"

"Everything *taxed* is indexed," Ren said. "They never paid."

Yuki looked at the boats, and Ren watched the word *umbrella* — her word, her whole creed — turn over in her face like a slate showing its back.

"The opening team moved up," she said at last, and the ruled voice came back, but it came back thinner. "The canal-end. The resident Hollow. Command wants it opened at dawn — before the dusk rain, before the threads, clean water, maximum control. Three seniors, an auditor, and me." Her hand went to the mask at her hip, face-down, never yet used, and rested there. "My first opening, Ishikari. They're giving it to me because it's small. A pooled shame-Hollow, second layer, no depth. The manual says four breaths and it's done."

"The manual," Ren said, "says Hollows are litter."

"I know what the manual says."

"And the water's been humming a six-year-old's tune for two days."

Yuki was quiet for a moment. The rain came down on the canal-end, on the flat water, on the twelve silent boats, and the thirteenth bell's weather finally, finally began to lift off the district, the way a held breath lifts.

"Ishikari," she said. "The manual says open. You said Hollows are questions. So here's mine, and I want it on the record that I'm asking it *before* dawn, with my slate off." She turned the dead slate over in her hands, once, like a coin. "If we open that water at dawn, and there's a child's unfinished grief in the pool — what happens to the *child*?"

And Ren Ishikari, who had spent four years not knowing what he had bought, looked at the flat water where a door waited under the rain, and heard the Ferryman's free gift turning over in him like a coin of his own, and said the truest thing he knew:

"I don't know. But I don't think we're the ones who get to find out safely."

The Guild boats poled away, one by one, without a sound, into the rain.
